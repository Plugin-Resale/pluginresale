Thomas van Opstal is a mixing and mastering engineer based in Amsterdam. At 21, he became head audio at Red Bull Music Studios Amsterdam, and later ran the same studio as a commercial facility under the name Atlantisstudio. Over the years he has recorded and mixed for artists like Wu-Tang Clan, FKJ and Naomi Sharon. Since 2021, he has also been posting daily videos about recording and mixing on Instagram, TikTok and YouTube.

We talked about how a bedroom FL Studio producer ended up running a Red Bull studio, why giving away studio time and selling it are two very different businesses, a live session in the marble hall of a museum, what content actually brings clients to an engineer, his analog-first mixing chain, and the 7,000 plugins on his system.

## Career

### How did you get into audio, and how did you end up as head audio at Red Bull Music Studios Amsterdam?

At the age of 13, I started making beats in FL Studio in my bedroom. At 16, I studied music production and took classes in recording and mixing. That's where my interest in audio engineering began. After graduating, I set up a studio in my mom's attic, watched every episode of Pensado's Place and was ready to take on project after project.

Unfortunately, things stayed pretty quiet and I wasn't getting the work I was expecting. I soon realised I was missing a crucial part needed to do this professionally: experience. I decided to spend my savings on the audio engineering course at SAE Institute in Amsterdam, to be able to work in a proper studio and finally gain the experience I needed.

I soon learned SAE had a collaboration with Red Bull, where a strict selection of the best SAE alumni could engineer sessions at the Red Bull studios. I worked my ass off and I was one of the lucky few to be selected. At the time, this was an unpaid work experience thing.

After a couple of months of doing sessions, Red Bull announced they were going to do a full rebuild of the studio, and I had apparently made such a good impression during those sessions that they asked me to apply for the job of head audio. With the competition being engineers from other studios with years of experience, and even my teacher from SAE, I didn't think I was going to be the one to get picked. But my young age, my relaxed vibe and my music taste lining up with the Red Bull team made them hire me in the end, and at 21 years old I became head audio of Red Bull Music Studios Amsterdam.

### What was the move from Red Bull Music Studios to running it as Atlantisstudio like?

The transition from Red Bull Music Studios to Atlantisstudio was a big one. Red Bull used the studio as a marketing tool, which meant none of the artists paid for studio time. When it became Atlantisstudio, it suddenly needed to run as a commercial facility: we now needed to bring in clients who were willing to pay for studio time.

I had built a big network during my time at Red Bull, but they weren't super excited to now pay to work in a studio they previously had access to for free. That was a big hurdle. We tried very hard to make the studio work as a business, but it didn't work out in the end. When my own mixing and mastering business took off and I had outgrown being a studio engineer and manager, it was time for a new challenge.

### You've worked with artists like Wu-Tang Clan, FKJ or Naomi Sharon. Is there a session or project you're especially proud of, or that taught you something?

I think I'm most proud of a session with a local artist here in Amsterdam, called RIMON. We did these live sessions called "UNCUT", and she wanted to do hers in the marble hall of the Tropenmuseum here in Amsterdam: the last place you'd set up a band, let alone a 16-piece one, and record a song.

For this session I had to take out half of the studio gear and set it up in a place no one in their right mind would set up a studio. I took Neumann tube mics, Coles ribbons, Neve, API and UA preamps and everything else needed to do a proper studio-grade recording on location. It was a very hectic and long day, but probably the best session I have ever done in my life.

## Content creation

### In 2021 you started posting daily on Instagram, TikTok and YouTube. What pushed you to do it, and how has it changed your career?

After working in high-end studios for years, I noticed a big interest from producers in the knowledge I had gained. I could talk about engineering for hours during a studio session, and I would. But when Instagram Reels and TikTok became a thing, I noticed I could share that knowledge with a much bigger audience at once, instead of one studio session at a time. It was amazing to see that people actually watched the videos I made, and that they had a positive impact on their careers.

### How do you balance studio work and content creation day to day?

There's not really a balance, to be honest. One week we're swamped with mixing, recording and mastering sessions, and the next we fly out to shoot content somewhere or rebuild the studio again. And that's exactly how I want to live my life. I like contrast, not only in the way I mix, but also in the way I live. I would hate it if I sat behind my desk doing the same thing every day. Things are very busy, though, so where possible we aim to combine creating content with working on music.

### Any advice for engineers who want to start making content?

Make sure you have a goal in mind. Do you want to create content to get more work as an engineer, or do you want to be a content creator or influencer? We're very focused on views, likes and follows, but the content that does well on that side doesn't necessarily translate into clients finding you and you getting more work as an engineer.

On the contrary: a very basic post about you working on song X for artist Y will often perform poorly engagement-wise, but it's exactly the thing a potential client needs to see when they're looking to hire you.

## Workflow

### How does a typical mix go for you, from receiving the session to delivery?

I start by loading the files into my template, and while I route everything where it needs to go, I listen to songs in the same genre: either references provided by the artist, or songs I feel could fit in the same playlist.

I usually start with vocals, running them through an analog chain, usually a Neve 1073 and the Avalon 747, or one of my vari-mu compressors. After that I move to bass and drums: kick and snare are usually on the E series channels of my mini SSL, and the bass on the G series. I try to fit as many sources as possible on the mini SSL so I can mix them together as one unit. That gives me the workflow and sonic coherence I'm looking for.

Once that's set, I move on to the instruments. The treatment there depends entirely on the type and character of each element in the song. From there on, I engage the clipper and limiters to see where we are LUFS-wise and how it compares to the references. I constantly monitor their gain reduction when making tweaks.

### Hybrid or in the box? How do you split your work between analog gear and plugins?

My analog gear gives me a hands-on workflow I really like, and that I'm used to from working on a big SSL console at Red Bull Music Studios all those years. It also gives me a depth plugins don't really have, and it helps massively when dealing with bad recordings, especially harshness.

The big analog setup also helps with wanting to do this for another 30 or 40 years: it feels a bit nicer than just doing everything in a DAW. If I wanted to sit behind a desk working in a computer program, I would've picked a job that paid a bit better than music, I think ;)

## Plugins

### What are the 3 to 5 plugins you couldn't work without, and why?

I'll get to the list, but first I want to say that throughout my career, I've made a point of not having any tools I couldn't mix without. I feel it's dangerous to rely too heavily on a plugin or a certain piece of gear: when there are license issues, your Mac is outdated or the gear is broken, you can't get that sound anymore. That's also why I try to stay away from plugins where I don't understand what's going on under the hood, the magic tools where one knob changes five parameters but you don't know exactly which.

Anyway, if I had to make a list of the plugins I use most:

- [SSL 4K E Channel Strip](/developers/solid-state-logic/ssl-4k-e)
- [Soundtoys EchoBoy](/developers/soundtoys/echoboy)
- [FabFilter Pro-Q](/developers/fabfilter)
- [Soundtoys Decapitator](/developers/soundtoys/decapitator)
- [ADPTR Audio Metric AB](/developers/plugin-alliance/metric-ab)

I recently found out I have over 7,000 plugins on my system... so, where do I start? Haha.

## Thomas's plugins, second-hand

With over 7,000 plugins on his system, Thomas is probably not the only one sitting on licenses he never opens. Here is what the developers say about reselling the plugins he uses most:

- **[SSL 4K E Channel Strip](/developers/solid-state-logic/ssl-4k-e)**: Solid State Logic doesn't handle transfers itself: the seller moves the license to the buyer's iLok User ID in iLok License Manager and pays iLok's transfer fee. The Production Pack bundled with SSL hardware can't be transferred. [SSL transfer rules](/developers/solid-state-logic).
- **Soundtoys [EchoBoy](/developers/soundtoys/echoboy) and [Decapitator](/developers/soundtoys/decapitator)**: the seller transfers the license to the buyer's iLok account in iLok License Manager ($25 iLok fee, paid by the seller), then emails Soundtoys support with the buyer's email and iLok ID so the registration is updated. Transfers need Soundtoys' written consent and can be refused. [Soundtoys transfer rules](/developers/soundtoys).
- **FabFilter Pro-Q ([Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4), [Pro-Q 3](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-3))**: about €10 for a License Transfer, bought by the seller in their FabFilter account, then the seller moves the license to the buyer's name and email. FabFilter treats each major version as a separate product, so an older Pro-Q can be sold on its own. Educational and NFR licenses can't be transferred. [FabFilter transfer rules](/developers/fabfilter).
- **[ADPTR Audio Metric AB](/developers/plugin-alliance/metric-ab)**: sold through Plugin Alliance, where self-service transfers are currently unavailable. Requests may be handled case by case by Native Instruments support (Plugin Alliance's owner). [Plugin Alliance transfer rules](/developers/plugin-alliance).
