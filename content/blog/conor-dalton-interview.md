Conor Dalton is the mastering engineer behind Glowcast Audio Mastering, a Berlin studio that has been finishing records for 15 years, mostly electronic and club music, but also indie, rock, hip-hop and ambient. He also teaches workshops at Abbey Road Institute and Berklee, and runs a free Discord where engineers and musicians share their chains and master the same tracks in shootouts.

We talked about why mastering always fascinated him, his signal flow, loudness for the club, the plugins he relies on, and the one piece of gear he couldn't do without.

## Career

### How did you get into music, and into mastering specifically?

I had many roles in the music industry before I settled into mastering. I was a DJ and producer, I played in bands, I put on club nights with an organisation and with friends, I worked at a record label, and I had part-time jobs in bars and restaurants on the side.

Mastering always interested me, from the first day of college. I remember a student asking the professor whether we would cover audio mastering in the course, and the professor said: "No, I don't think so really, mastering feels a bit too advanced for this level." I was immediately fascinated: if mastering seems so mysterious and so distant, what is it? I think subconsciously I always gravitated towards it and wanted to move into that field.

I love sitting at the end of the creative process, receiving music and taking it to the finish line. I like the fast pace. I like not being stuck with one song for hours. I like being involved in the creativity, but not in the chaotic nature of recording. I feel at home in mastering.

### Tell us about Glowcast: what is it, and what kind of clients and genres do you work with?

Glowcast Audio Mastering is a Berlin-based mastering studio. We mostly specialise in electronic and club music, but we also work on a lot of other styles: singer-songwriter and indie, rock, hip-hop, ambient.

We are a small team of three. There's me, the main mastering engineer; Seb, who works with stems; and M'hamed, the office manager, who helps with administration and project preparation. We've been in business for 15 or 16 years.

I also teach workshops in different cities. Audio education is part of our services and very important to me, but mastering makes up 90 to 95% of my work.

### A recent project you're particularly proud of, and why?

I'm proud of the community space I created on my [Discord](https://discord.gg/KQcWs5K23Z). It's a place where mastering engineers, mixing engineers and musicians can hang out, learn from each other and help each other level up. It's a free resource and a growing community, and it has helped all of us sharpen our skills.

Sometimes we do shootouts: we all master the same song, vote for our favourites and share our processing chains. It's been really fun, and it's open to the public. It's good to have a place of learning with no gatekeeping. I share everything there openly, along with many other talented engineers doing the same.

## Workflow

### What's your current setup: monitors, room? Fully in the box, or some analog hardware?

I monitor on ATC speakers with subs and a Trinnov. In my hardware chain I have an Elysia nvelope, a Thermionic Culture Phoenix Mastering Plus, a Shadow Hills compressor, a Black Box HG-2, a Chandler Curve Bender, a Knif Soma EQ, a Dangerous Music BAX EQ, and a Manley Vari-Mu compressor with a Dutch Audio IM2.3.

I work hybrid, analogue and digital, and my workflow is always evolving. Sometimes I work fully digital: if there's already a lot of analogue processing in the mix, I use cleaner, more transparent tools to take it to the finish line. I also have a pair of PMC MB2-XBD speakers in storage, which will go into my next studio.

### Walk us through a typical master: what do you listen for first, and what's your usual chain?

I try not to have a typical chain. I start every project with a neutral mindset, without a preconceived idea of what I'm going to do. I play the song and resist the urge to start making plans and applying processing immediately. I try to gather more data and context, to develop an understanding of the song, and then, when the time is right, I choose my processing and formulate my ideas.

The equipment changes with the needs of the song, but the philosophy stays similar: first the corrective processes, then the controlling processes, then the colourful processes, and then limiting and dither. I don't always work through those stages in a linear way, I can jump between processors in whatever order I wish, but in terms of signal flow, that's my general philosophy.

Restraint is as important as action in mastering, so I try to do as little as possible, but as much as I need. Most of the work is done with EQ. I try not to over-compress, and when I'm using clippers and limiters, I prefer several limiters each doing a tiny bit of work rather than one limiter that's struggling.

### Loudness: how do you deal with clients who want it louder, now that streaming normalises everything?

Loudness is still desirable, but we have to make sure we aren't introducing damage, distortion and stress into the song to achieve it. Loudness can be done in a very tasteful and satisfying way. I definitely enjoy loud, fat-sounding music, as long as the transients haven't been smashed to get there.

A lot of my clients care more about how their music will sound in the club than on streaming platforms. The most important thing is that the song sounds good at any volume, on every system. It's the mastering engineer's responsibility to recognise when the pursuit of loudness starts to become a problem, and then to retreat to something more sensible. As Ian Shepherd would say, the final LUFS of a song is the result of the mastering, not the goal of the mastering.

### What makes a mix easy (or hard) to master? One tip for people sending you their mixes?

A mix is easy to master when people either know exactly what they want, or trust you to pursue your own vision for the song. It's harder when the musician has a sound in mind but couldn't (or didn't) apply the processing that would move the song towards it.

If the song has density, balance and richness while keeping the punch and power of the drums, that's a very good place to start. And you always learn a lot by simply asking the musician: "Are you happy with the mix?" The answer changes how you act as the mastering engineer.

### Is there a plugin you find overrated, or one you bought and never used?

There are many I bought and never used, or stopped using. Not because there's anything wrong with them, but because I found better options for my needs, for a variety of reasons. I have a very specific set of requirements, so it's hard to find tools that really satisfy my working needs. I'm always hunting for improved tools and secret weapons, and I like to stay as up to date as I can.

## Today

### What are you working on right now?

I'm working on a book, but taking my time. I'm mastering music, teaching workshops at Abbey Road Institute and Berklee, and this year I'm taking part in People Festival in Berlin: a collaboration between more than 100 musicians who create music together for a week and then perform it for the public in one-time performances. I'll be travelling in the coming months, and I'm always meeting new musicians and making new connections.

### One piece of advice for someone mastering their own music at home?

If you're mastering at home, I assume you won't have the best acoustics and monitoring. In that case I'd recommend world-class headphones and an amp, for example Audeze LCD-5S and an RME ADI-2 Pro FS R Black Edition. That's not cheap, maybe 7k or more, but it's a lot cheaper than building a professional studio from scratch, and it will take you much, much further in your career than cheap speakers in bad acoustics.

Hearing the audio clearly is the most important thing you can spend your money on, much more important than buying hardware. I could work without all of my hardware, but not without my headphones. I could work without my speakers and my studio if I had to, but not without my headphones. They've become the most important piece of gear I own, even though I also spend a lot of time on speakers.

## Conor's plugins, second-hand

Conor is always moving on to better tools, which means licenses he no longer uses. Here is what the developers say about reselling the plugins he mentions:

- **[FabFilter Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4)**: about €10 for a License Transfer, bought by the seller in their FabFilter account, then the seller moves the license to the buyer. FabFilter treats each major version as a separate product, so an older [Pro-Q](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-3) can be sold on its own. [FabFilter transfer rules](/developers/fabfilter).
- **[Softube Weiss DS1-MK3](/developers/softube/weiss-ds1-mk3)**: transferred through iLok ($25 per license or bundle, max $50 per order), not before 90 days after the license was deposited. Plugins that are part of locked bundles can't be sold separately. [Softube transfer rules](/developers/softube).
- **Sonnox [Oxford Inflator](/developers/sonnox/oxford-inflator) and [Oxford Limiter](/developers/sonnox/oxford-limiter)**: the seller moves the license in iLok License Manager and pays the iLok fee (capped at $50 for several licenses to the same account), no Sonnox fee. Full licenses only, at least 90 days old, no NFR. [Sonnox transfer rules](/developers/sonnox).
- **DMG Audio Equilibrium**: £20, paid by the seller through the transfer form on DMG Audio's support page. A transferred license can't be transferred again, and EDU licenses can't be resold. [DMG Audio transfer rules](/developers/dmg-audio).
- **[TDR Kotelnikov GE](/developers/tokyo-dawn-records/kotelnikov-ge)**: Tokyo Dawn's license agreement forbids selling a license without its prior written consent, so ask TDR first. The free Kotelnikov can't be resold anyway. [Tokyo Dawn transfer rules](/developers/tokyo-dawn-records).
- **Schwabe Digital HiFAL**: Schwabe Digital does not recognise or support license transfers. [Schwabe Digital transfer rules](/developers/schwabe-digital).

Naturl Audio, Hacienda Audio, SIR Audio Tools and AOM aren't in our list yet.
