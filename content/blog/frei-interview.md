FREI wears a lot of hats. He produces, sings, raps, records, mixes and masters, sometimes all on the same song. He learned the studio as an intern at Studios Ferber in Paris, where he assisted on sessions for artists like Manu Chao, Pomme, Philippe Katerine, Camélia Jordana and Vincent Segal. He then learned to produce hip-hop in Los Angeles and spent time in Madrid. Today he lives between Paris and Luxembourg, producing artists across Europe. Along the way he has also mixed for a Netflix production.

We asked him about his path, his setup, how he keeps perspective on his own songs, and the plugins he can't do without.

## Career

### How did you get into music, and into sound engineering?

My father introduced me to music. He was a guitarist, bassist and pianist, and I spent a lot of my childhood watching him perform. When I was six, he enrolled me in music school, where I started learning the saxophone.

At home, I was constantly listening to music. I would play CDs by artists like Madonna and Alpha Blondy over and over again. Music was always part of my life, both through my father and through my own curiosity.

I got into sound engineering after high school. I had applied to several university programs and wasn't accepted, so I decided to explore sound engineering. What started as a plan B quickly became a real passion: it brought together my interest in music, technology and collaboration.

### Paris, Los Angeles, Madrid. How has each place shaped the way you work?

Each place has influenced a different part of my artistic and technical approach.

Paris gave me my musical foundations and introduced me to the professional studio world. My internship at Studios Ferber taught me discipline, attention to detail, and the importance of understanding every stage of a session.

Los Angeles taught me how to produce hip-hop, and helped me understand the culture around the music and how it's made. It also pushed me to become more independent and more confident in my creative decisions.

Madrid opened me up to Hispanic culture and a completely different musical environment, with new rhythms, sounds and ways of collaborating. Living in different places has made me more adaptable and open-minded as a producer and engineer.

### You've assisted on sessions for Manu Chao, Pomme, Philippe Katerine, Camélia Jordana and Vincent Segal. What did you learn as an assistant that you still use today?

The most important lesson was that teamwork is a major factor in the success of any project. As an assistant, I learned that a session isn't only about technical knowledge. It's also about preparation, communication, organisation, and making sure everyone can work in the best possible conditions.

I also learned to anticipate people's needs, stay calm under pressure, and stay attentive to the artist's vision. Those habits still guide me today, whether I'm assisting someone or leading a whole project.

### You're a producer, singer, rapper and engineer. How do you switch hats? Is it hard to mix your own music?

I see myself as a tool for the project. My goal is to be as useful and efficient as possible, so I use whichever skill serves the music best.

Switching roles can be challenging, especially on my own music. As an artist, I'm emotionally connected to the song. As an engineer, I need to stay objective and make technical decisions.

To keep perspective, I separate the different stages of the process. I focus first on the creative and emotional side, then take a step back before editing, mixing or mastering. Getting feedback from people I trust helps too.

## Workflow

### What's your current setup: DAW, monitors, room? Fully in the box, or some hardware?

My main DAW is Logic Pro. For vocals, my recording chain is a CM800T microphone into a Neve 1073, an SSL 611 and an RME UFX III interface.

I monitor on Prosodia Bora speakers. I mix in a studio where I designed and built the acoustic treatment myself, which gave me a real understanding of how the room affects what I hear.

Once the recording is done, I work fully in the box. I prefer it because it gives me flexibility, instant recall, and precise control throughout the mix.

### On some projects you handle everything, from recording and production to mixing, mastering and Atmos. Walk us through one of them.

Let's take "Rendez-Vous" by Ferran Sinatra.

I start by talking with the artist to understand the intention, the references and the emotional direction of the song. Then we sit together and build the chords and the foundation of the track. After that, I record the main vocals and make sure we have a strong, complete vocal performance.

Once the recording is finished, I send the artist home and work on the arrangement and the first mix. When the structure and the mix are in a good place, we go back to the vocal production and refine the details.

Finally, I master the track in stereo and in Atmos.

To keep perspective, I try to define the identity of the song as early as possible. Every production, mixing and mastering decision should support that identity. Experience plays a big role too: the more projects you finish, the easier it is to recognise when a song is moving in the right direction.

### You've mixed for a Netflix production. What changes between mixing for picture and mixing a single?

It's a different approach. The sessions are usually much bigger, with more tracks, versions, edits, dialogue, sound effects and music. And the mix has to serve the story, the images and the director's intentions, rather than existing on its own.

The expectations are different too, technically and creatively. You have to pay close attention to continuity, intelligibility, dynamics, and how the music sits with the dialogue and the sound design. The process usually takes longer and means working closely with the wider post-production team.

### Your bio says you help artists "shape their sonic identity". What does that look like in practice?

It means helping an artist develop a sound that feels authentic, recognisable and coherent.

I don't only focus on the technical quality of a recording. I also help artists make artistic decisions about their arrangements, vocal approach, production, textures, references and overall direction.

Sometimes that means encouraging an artist to try something unfamiliar. Other times, it means stripping the production back so the essential character of the song comes through. My role is to connect the music, the artist's personality and the wider image of the project.

## Plugins

### What are your must-have plugins, and why?

- **Waves C1**: simple.
- **Soundtoys Little AlterBoy**: unique.
- **Soundtoys MicroShift**: satisfying.
- **FabFilter Pro-Q 3**: precise.

They let me solve common problems quickly while still leaving room for creative decisions.

### Is there a plugin you find overrated, or one you bought and never used?

Waves Harmony is probably the one I've used the least. It's not necessarily a bad plugin, it just never became part of my regular workflow.

I try not to judge plugins only on their reputation. A tool can be excellent and still not be the right fit for a particular workflow or musical style.

### How do you buy plugins: full price, sales, bundles, second-hand? Have you ever resold a license?

Either at full price when I need something for a project, or during sales when I'm planning ahead.

I try to be selective rather than buying every new release. I want to know what problem a plugin will solve, and whether it will really improve my workflow.

I've never resold a license.

## Today

### What are you working on right now?

I'm producing several artists across Europe while continuing to build my portfolio. I'm focused on artists with a strong identity, helping them turn their ideas into complete, coherent projects. I'm also still developing my own work as a producer, vocalist, engineer and mixer.

### What advice would you give to a young producer or engineer working from a home studio?

You don't need an expensive studio to start making great music. A good pair of headphones, a reliable microphone and a solid audio interface are enough to start developing your skills.

I'd also say: invest time in your ears before investing heavily in equipment. Learn your room, understand your monitoring, finish songs, and ask for feedback.

I've invested around €30,000 in my studio, but that happened gradually, over years of experience. The equipment should support your development, not become a substitute for it.

## FREI's plugins, second-hand

All four of FREI's must-haves can be resold, and each developer has its own transfer process:

- **[Waves C1 Compressor](/browse?q=C1%20Compressor)**: Waves charges a transfer fee of 5% of the product's list price (min $10, max $150), paid by the seller, and the license needs an active Waves Update Plan. Details in our [Waves transfer guide](/blog/how-to-transfer-a-waves-license).
- **[Little AlterBoy](/browse?q=Little%20AlterBoy)** and **[MicroShift](/browse?q=MicroShift)**: Soundtoys licenses move through iLok ($25 fee, paid by the seller), then Soundtoys support updates the registration. [Soundtoys transfer rules](/developers/soundtoys).
- **[FabFilter Pro-Q 3](/browse?q=Pro-Q%203)**: FabFilter charges about €10 for a transfer, bought by the seller in their FabFilter account. Pro-Q 3 is a separate product from Pro-Q 4 (FabFilter charges for major upgrades), so a second-hand Pro-Q 3 can be a cheaper way to get the EQ FREI calls precise. [FabFilter transfer rules](/developers/fabfilter).
