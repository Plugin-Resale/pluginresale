Emilie Daelemans is a mastering engineer based in Maisons-Alfort, just outside Paris. She interned with Josh Bonati in New York and spent several years at Studio Ferber in Paris, first as an intern and then as an assistant, before setting up her own studio. She has mastered records for Agar Agar, Mocky, Bonnie Banane and Flavien Berger, and she is a musician herself, with her project Lianor.

We talked about how she got into mastering, what she learned in Brooklyn, her hybrid chain and why compression stays analog, the plugins she relies on, and how playing music shapes the way she listens to a mix.

## Getting started

### How did you get into sound in the first place?

I began playing drums when I was 8, and my passion for music started with playing with friends. I quickly knew I didn't want to become a professional musician, but I really liked the texture of sound. So I started recording some demos, and after high school I enrolled in a sound engineering school.

### What made you choose mastering over recording or mixing?

In mastering, I found the precision and the delicacy I like to work with. I really enjoy how 1 dB can change perception and intensity. I also like being part of the final step and offering the listener the best experience possible.

### You spent some time in Brooklyn assisting Josh Bonati. What did you learn there that you still use today?

I learned to work on so many formats, such as vinyl, cassette and DAT. I discovered the importance of being patient enough to find the right way to approach each project.

I also learned a lot about gear and how a mastering studio is set up. At the time, I had never used analog mastering gear, and Josh helped me understand how the machines react with each other, and how many different possibilities there can be with the same setup.

## The studio

### What are you listening on these days, and how long did it take you to really trust it?

I'm listening on a pair of Amphion Two18. Adapting to them was quick, because I used to work on them during my assistant years at Studio Ferber. I really like the clarity and the simplicity of their sound.

### What does your mastering chain look like? What's analog, and what stays in the box?

I use a hybrid setup. It took me a while to find the right balance in the analog realm between efficiency, accuracy and the color I was looking for.

Plugins are very good for surgical work, or to try gear I can't afford yet... Ahah. These days there are so many good emulations, there's been a lot of progress in the past 10 years.

Actually, I mostly use plugins when I need to do subtractive work, or as a limiter at the end of the chain.

For me, compression is the hardest process to do solely in digital. I've never found the musical glue with a plugin, whereas it's quick and efficient on analog gear.

Sometimes I think that what I do in analog could be done in the digital domain. I'm aware that what I really like in analog machines is that there are some things you can't control: the electronic components live a life of their own. I guess that's what we call "magic" when we talk about analog sound.

## Plugins

### Which plugins could you not work without?

- [FabFilter Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4)
- [Oxford Inflator](/developers/sonnox/oxford-inflator)
- Metric Halo Sontec MES-432D9D
- [TDR Arbiter](/developers/tokyo-dawn-records/arbiter)
- [FabFilter Pro-L 2](/developers/fabfilter/pro-l-2)

### Is there a plugin you find overrated, or one you bought and never really used?

I can't hear another word about [Soothe](/developers/oeksound/soothe2)... Ahah.

### How do you buy plugins and gear: full price, during sales, second-hand?

I buy plugins during sales, because there are sales all the time...

My gear has mostly been bought second-hand, but I sometimes buy new gear directly from the manufacturer. It allows me to ask for some adjustments on the machine, and I really like having a direct connection with the maker. The audio world, and mastering especially, is so specific that it's possible to work directly with other kinds of collaborators, such as machine manufacturers.

## The job

### How does a project usually start with you? What do you ask the artist or the mixer before you begin?

I ask how they feel about the mixes, and whether they expect a transparent master or would rather bring in a new color.

I also like to receive some references. Sometimes they don't find the words to explain what they expect from mastering. I'm trying to understand what the vibe is, and what emotions they want to communicate through their sound choices.

### What's the most common problem in the mixes you receive, and what should people do before sending a track to mastering?

Most of the problems I find in mixes come from speakers that are too generous in the low end, and also, I guess, from acoustic problems in the mixing environment.

The low end is always difficult to manage.

### How do you deal with loudness, and with clients who want it louder?

Focusing on loudness is old school. I'm bored with that.

### You're also a musician. Does playing music change the way you master?

Of course! It helps me a lot to understand what the musicians meant to do with their instruments.

You wouldn't be able to understand why this snare or that bass is so crushed, loud or wide if you had never tried to create emotions this way yourself.

When I listen to a new mix, I analyze it from a sonic perspective, but then I take the time to find out what the groove is and what it wants to say.

You can instantly destroy the groove of a song if you make the wrong compression choice.

I guess I would never have approached mastering the way I do if I didn't play music myself.

## Today

### Is there a record you're especially proud of? Why that one?

I'm proud of a few records. If I have to pick one, I would say Agar Agar, *The Dog and the Future*.

I'm a fan of the way they use electronic instruments with such organic vibes. They have a unique groove, and it's very powerful. That made my work very pleasant, and I was very honored to add one more color to this album.

### What are you working on at the moment?

I'm thrilled to be working with English bands!

All the information is on my website, and you can find me on Instagram for the famous "Mastered" stories :)

## Emilie's plugins, second-hand

Here is what the developers say about reselling the plugins she can't work without:

- **[FabFilter Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4)** and **[Pro-L 2](/developers/fabfilter/pro-l-2)**: transferable. The seller buys a License Transfer (about €10) in their FabFilter account, then transfers the license to the buyer's name and email. Educational and NFR licenses can't be transferred, and bundles only as a whole. [FabFilter transfer rules](/developers/fabfilter).
- **[Sonnox Oxford Inflator](/developers/sonnox/oxford-inflator)**: transferable through iLok License Manager. The seller pays the iLok fee (no Sonnox fee), the license must be a full license at least 90 days old, and Sonnox then sends re-registration instructions to the buyer. [Sonnox transfer rules](/developers/sonnox).
- **Metric Halo Sontec MES-432D9D**: Metric Halo licenses are transferred to the buyer's iLok account in iLok License Manager. The seller pays the iLok fee ($25 per license or bundle, $50 for several in one transaction), Metric Halo charges nothing on top. NFR licenses and licenses bundled with hardware are generally excluded. [Metric Halo transfer rules](/developers/metric-halo).
- **[TDR Arbiter](/developers/tokyo-dawn-records/arbiter)**: can't be resold. Tokyo Dawn Records' license agreement forbids selling licenses without its written consent. [Tokyo Dawn Records transfer rules](/developers/tokyo-dawn-records).

The Sontec MES-432D9D isn't in our list yet. Metric Halo is, so when you sell, you can add it yourself from the [Sell form](/sell). And if you're looking for one of these plugins, search for it on [Browse](/browse) and set an alert: we'll email you as soon as someone lists it.
