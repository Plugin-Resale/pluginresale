Anotyne Nouel is a producer, mixing and mastering engineer and an artist. She interned at Sundlaugin, Sigur Rós's studio in Iceland, built Sound Love Studio in Lyon with her own hands, and now works from Marseille, mostly on a pair of Audeze headphones. She works mainly with women and queer artists, and she has been touring with bands since 2011.

We talked about Iceland, learning without a mentor, mastering on headphones, recording "almost mastered", the plugins she relies on, and what needs to change for women and queer people in studios.

## Career

### You spent six months at Sigur Rós's studio. How did that come about, and what did you take away from it?

It was thirteen years ago actually. I needed to do an internship at the end of my studies. I applied to Sundlaugin and was interviewed by the assistant there at the time. I think I showed enough interest, because they took me.

Sigur Rós's studio is really the nicest production studio I've ever been to. They had all the mics (U47s, U67s, AEA R84s...), so many guitars, amps, synths, pianos, organs. It was really crazy. They had a lot of cool outboard too, like a Roland RE-201 tape delay, a Manley Massive Passive, a Smart C2 compressor, an AMS RMX16 reverb...

I spent a lot of time at the studio when there were no sessions, mixing and mastering my own projects. I did a really cool experiment: I composed and recorded an album in my bedroom in Reykjavik with only an SM57, a Casio SK-1 toy synth and a broken electric guitar I couldn't plug in, and used Sigur Rós's studio to mix and master it. It's called *Let's Freeze Our Feelings and Split the Hate*. I still love it. I used the Neve console there to sum my mixes, with the Massive Passive and the Smart C2 on the master bus on every song. That AMS reverb was so nice, it was on all the mixes. We also had Dynaudio BM15As. I really liked them in that room, everything I did there sounded really nice.

Spending that much time in a pro studio is what showed me I was made to live this life. I was there almost every day, either on my own or with Birgir Jón Birgisson, the studio's main engineer. He even offered me the job of studio assistant at the end, but my first band had just started touring and I really wanted to go back to my life in France. Being able to hear all those instruments, mics and effects really helped me make my choices later on.

Something I mention quite often: in reality, I was really bored there. I remember a session where we were recording Kjartan, Sigur Rós's former keyboard player, and a bassist. I was on the couch at the back of the control room and Biggi filmed me falling asleep. They were recording ambient music. When you're an intern, most of the time you have nothing to do, you're just listening in silence, and nobody really needs your opinion. But what I realized is that being there, even in silence, really shaped me. I didn't even notice, but I was learning so much.

### You've said you learned most of it on your own. What was that like, and what would have helped?

After Iceland, I really wanted to work on records. Maybe because I hung out a lot with the punk scene, I was always in a DIY mindset. I wanted to be in control, I wanted to mix, I wanted to master. Being an assistant or an intern again felt frustrating. I just wanted to go straight to the engineer's seat, even on smaller projects.

I should also say that I'm a trans person. Even if I could be perceived as non-binary, I identify more with femininity. I only understood this much later in life, in 2022, but I think it really disconnected me from the guys doing sound work around me. I never felt like them, I could never really connect with them, and I could never set up a studio project with them. So after my internship I started doing a lot of sound work on my own. I turned my bedroom into a studio.

Having a good mentor is the best and fastest way to become a good engineer. But it's 98% men. When you're a girl or a queer person, spending all your time with guys can be difficult. I never could.

The second best way to learn, I'd say, is practice. Trial and error. That's what I did. I've heard mastering engineers say you need 10,000 hours of mastering before you're really a mastering engineer, and I think that may be close to the truth.

A third way is to learn as much as you can from videos of expert engineers. Not the ones who don't really work in the field and just talk: the ones who actually make the records we listen to. I'll confess that at one point I was really frustrated. I had built my studio, I had my ATCs and my sub, but something wasn't quite right with my masters. I wanted to be as good as the best American engineers, and I had put so much effort into the room and the speakers. I ended up watching all the mastering videos on Mix with the Masters. I don't remember if that's where I found my answer, but seeing how the best in the game work definitely helped me fine-tune my workflow, and understand that at some point you have to build your own personal recipe. I also watched most of the MixCon videos on mixing, which taught me a lot of new techniques and ideas. I'm always keen to watch new videos about producing, mixing or mastering. There are so many approaches, and it's always interesting to understand how other people work.

## From Lyon to Marseille

### In Lyon, you built Sound Love Studio with your own hands from an acoustician's plans. What does it look like in Marseille?

I always told people around me that nowadays you could engineer sound with just a pair of headphones. When I lost my studio and had to go back to a home studio, where I started, I wanted to try what I had been advising everyone. I couldn't just put my ATC SCM25A and SCS70 sub in my home, it would have been far too intense for the people I live with and for the neighbours. So I put every penny I had into a perfect headphone monitoring system.

I bought a second-hand pair of Audeze LCD-5 and started experimenting from there. Coming from ATC speakers, a known reference in mastering, I wanted the most precise headphones I could find so my work wouldn't suffer from the change. I also bought good headphone amps and ended up with a Violectric 550 Pro. I've always considered monitoring the most important piece of gear in the studio. I use it all with no EQ or correction at all. Being sure of what you're hearing matters most. Everything else is optional to me.

Working on headphones is a bit different, and it took me some time to adapt, but today I'm actually happier with the sound I make on them than with my previous speakers and room. There's something really powerful about controlling the sound with your ear that close to the drivers. It's like the closest possible nearfield, but with the sound of main monitors (5 Hz to 50 kHz on the LCD-5). Once you're used to it, it's really hard to make a mistake, and it translates so well to headphones, earbuds...

I also know that the most expensive mastering rooms, like Sterling Sound, built from Northward Acoustics plans, are designed like big natural headphones, with the sound coming from the speakers fully absorbed at the back of the room. Working on some of the best headphones there are is, in theory, the same thing at a fraction of the price. I'm really happy with my choice, even if I'd be happy to use both speakers and headphones at some point.

### You see the studio as an instrument, and you'd rather produce than "just press record". What does that look like when an artist or a band comes in?

I always try to make it a fun process. I don't think recording should be annoying or boring for anyone. It should be fun, experimental, creative, fast, almost instinctive. For me, recording is almost like channeling something divine. Music is an old ritual humans perform. When I compose, I can be in a really special state. I lose track of time, I forget to eat or even to pee. I'm so absorbed by what I'm doing that nothing else exists except the energy, the sounds, the vibe, what I want to add next, where the moment is taking me.

So I try to bring the way I compose into everything I do on other people's tracks. I work with a lot of women and queer people, and I see those moments as sacred time to listen to them, where they should be fully heard and cared for. I think feeling good is deeply important to making great art.

I apply the same rule to myself, in a way. I want to be creative, I want to have a purpose, I want to be useful when I work on music. I don't want people coming to me just because I have expensive mics and preamps. I'd rather lend them to the artists I work with than just sit there hitting record for hours. I need a creative purpose. I want my vision to be needed.

In practice, a bit like a film director, I sometimes offer to record an artist because I have an idea of how they could sound through me. I went to see Js Donny perform their music on classical guitar and thought: that's something I should work on. I had already made an album of classical guitar, *Petits Animaux Blessés*, my own favourite album, so I had a lot of experience and ideas for that kind of material. I chose to produce that album with that specific guitar and loved the sound we ended up with. I lent them Space Echos, because I knew classical guitar and Space Echo are a killer combo. That album, *Death Folk*, is beautiful, and I made all the sound on it with Js, from production to mixing and mastering.

## Workflow

### What's your setup today? What made the trip from Lyon, and what stays in the computer?

Today I use my LCD-5s most of the time, but I also have NS10s, which I deeply love. Unlike my ATCs, those speakers never betrayed me: they always tell me when something is wrong in a mix or a master, even in the low end. They're a really good reality check. I also use them in mono, turned down about 10 dB, to compare songs with each other and bring them to the same perceived level. I had a console, a "Trident" Oram 8T16, that I used for summing, but it hasn't made it to Marseille yet. It takes a lot of space and I don't feel it's necessary anymore.

For DAWs, I use Pro Tools for mixing and mastering, WaveLab for editing albums (and sometimes for digital mastering), and FL Studio for composing these days. I like switching DAWs depending on the task. I have an Avid S1 with motorized faders, which I love using in Pro Tools: I can dial my plugins by hand, it almost feels like analog gear, and I make crazier, more personal moves with it.

I used Ableton Live for composing for a long time, but at some point I noticed I enjoyed the music made in FL Studio more, so I tried it and now I'm fully into it. I have a template and I can make a beat in an hour. It's so fast, the pattern mode is far more musical, and the step sequencer is a must-have for me. When I change something in a pattern, it applies to every copy of that pattern in the song, which saves a lot of time and energy. I bought an MPC Live II at some point but sent it back. FL Studio sits somewhere between a machine and a DAW, and that feels more musical to me.

My outboard has shrunk since Lyon. I still have a Prism Titan as my main converter, and some Hacienda Labs Studer preamps and EQs for tracking. I also record through a Bettermaker 232P: my Fender Precision bass sounds amazing through it, and I can recall the analog preset instantly. For mastering, I have a small analog chain: a Sontec DR-MQ5 parametric EQ, a Manley Massive Passive tube EQ and an Alan Smart C1 compressor. I use it on almost everything, but I also have a lot of really useful plugins.

### You often work with your eyes closed and the screen off, riding the volume on every track and even on the master. How did you get there, and what does it change?

I used to ride volumes a lot in Lyon, but I've kind of stopped. I still find it interesting, but for some reason it was harder to work that way on headphones. Maybe because I hear smaller moves? A 0.1 dB change can feel like a lot to me now. Maybe I'll come back to it if I have a room with my ATCs again. Maybe it's easier with some air between you and the speakers. It does sound nice, but it's really unusual: it creates moving textures. The mix evolves a lot between sections, and it can be a bit strange, because no two choruses sound the same, their levels depend on where my hands ended up at that moment. It's really organic, but it's not always the sound I'm after anymore.

I still really like working with my eyes closed or the screen off, though. I think that as musicians and engineers, we rely far too much on visuals. I also A/B a lot, switching limiters or bypassing effects for example. I want to be sure that whatever I tried really sounds better to my ear, because it's easy to get fooled. It has to be perfectly level-matched, within 0.1 dB, because louder always sounds better. Working that way made me rely much more on my ears than on my eyes, and now I focus more easily on the sound than on the graphics, even with my eyes open. It's good training: you can go further and it helps you focus and find your sweet spot.

### You like to record almost "mastered", with EQ and compression on the way in. Why commit so early?

Why not? Why wait? I want the best possible sound as soon as possible. If it means less work at the mixing and mastering stages, that's perfect to me. And the "mistakes" I made at those stages taught me a lot. I've definitely overcompressed a vocal, for example, but how would I know what that feels like if I had never tried? Undercompressed vocals feel like a problem to me too. I even feel converters work best when the sound is even. Analog gear sounds better to me, so it's more interesting to process the signal well at the very start than to reach for plugins later.

I don't really think of EQ or compression during tracking as committing. For me it's shaping. The sound I put in the computer should already be as close as possible to what I want. Less work, cleaner sound. Imagine stacking clean, compressed, EQ'd vocals: it's much easier to listen to early on. Same for bass: why record a clean DI? I use my Pultec-style Bettermaker 232P like a bass amp, to find the sound I'm after. Please commit!

## Plugins

### What are your must-have plugins, for mixing and for mastering?

I have so many. For mixing, we use a lot of them. I'm really into reverbs, I have so many it's a bit insane.

For mixing, one of my favourite plugins is actually Arturia's TAPE-201. It's my favourite delay, and I love delays. I love that you can spread it in stereo, and I almost always do. I recently got the [UAD Massive Passive](/developers/universal-audio/manley-massive-passive) and it's brilliant. I've owned the hardware for years and used it on everything, so I know it by heart, but recalling it is honestly a pain. Being able to just drop the plugin on the master bus is easier, even if it doesn't sound quite the same. I like the mastering version of the plugin too. I still use the real one for mastering.

For mastering, I don't use many. On my chain right now, almost always: Weiss Deess (de-esser), [ATR-102](/developers/universal-audio/ampex-atr-102-mastering-tape-recorder) (tape), StageOne 2 (stereo expander), [HiFAL](/developers/schwabe-digital/hifal-2) (high-frequency limiter) and Sima (clipper). For limiters, I mostly rely on [Uni-L](/developers/tone-projects/uni-l-master-limiter) and AL-1 now. I also like the Sontec MES-432D9D when I have M/S work to do or when I master digitally. And I sometimes use [Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4) when different parts of a song need different EQ.

### The Manley Massive Passive has been on your mixes since your Sigur Rós days, and you know its emulations well. Where do plugins win for you, and where do they fall short?

It's true, I've known that piece of gear since 2013, and it took me many years to be able to buy one. I really like plugins when I need to be fast and able to recall quickly and efficiently, like for mixing. I mix everything in the box nowadays, recalls are too much of a hassle. But sometimes I'll use just one piece of hardware on my master, like the Smart C1 compressor. I did that on my new album.

As for the Massive Passive plugin (I have UAD's version), I know from years of use that turning the knobs on the unit and moving them with a mouse in the plugin doesn't sound the same. It does sound similar, there's something about it that's really well captured. But the hardware sounds brighter and deeper. I have more options too: I can use the outputs with or without the Manley transformers.

I think there's something about analog gear that can't be captured. I saw an interview with Burgess Macneal of Sontec when they released the MES-432 plugin, saying they could never fully reproduce what the circuit does to the sound. I've never been into electronics, so I wouldn't understand why, but I could see in his eyes that however close we get, it will never sound the same. And maybe that's fine.

### How do you buy plugins and gear: full price, sales, second-hand? Have you ever bought or resold a used license?

For now, I buy plugins on sale when I can, or at full price. I've never bought or resold a license. I definitely should: I have a lot of plugins I don't use that I could sell, I guess. And I could definitely look at used licenses more when I want to buy new ones. I think a trusted website for that is a really good idea. Maybe that's what was missing: a plugin resale website that looks nice.

I almost always buy my hardware second-hand. I can find gear for up to a third of the new price, and on expensive gear that's thousands of euros. Good-quality gear doesn't age badly. When I was younger and buying cheaper gear, I had a lot of problems, because lower-quality gear can simply stop working after a while. I'm really careful now, I've made many bad bets.

## The scene

### Tell us about Sous-mixées, the conference you did in Marseille with a producer, an engineer and an artist. What came out of it?

I love that project and those people. We got together with the artist Janis and the producer of her record, Chris, aka Confuse, and talked about how we worked on that record. I did the mixing.

It was really interesting: for all of us, our experience of the music industry as queer women had been really violent. We went through a lot, and at some point we all had to distance ourselves from it. In an industry that's 98% men and considered technical, we're outnumbered and not treated as equals. As we said in the conference, a queer person or a woman working in this field has to be two or three times better than a man to be considered as good. That's hard when you're compared to top-tier engineers.

There's also the glass ceiling. As a trans woman running a studio, I went through extremely traumatic and violent events. I lost my studio, which I used to think of as my baby (it took me nine months to build). I think having a studio as a trans woman, in a place where people could see it, was above the glass ceiling for someone like me. We're not allowed to thrive, we're not allowed to have power. At some point the ceiling came crashing down and I lost everything. Men don't go through that. We're not equal, they have an advantage.

The conference will be available to everyone soon. It was a deep and important conversation about how to rebuild our lives as "minorities" who still want to make a living from music. One of the ways we found to cope was to team up and build careers together. We're stronger and harder to break when we have each other's backs.

### Women and non-binary people are still a tiny share of studio engineers. What needs to change, and what do you do about it in your own studio?

On men's side, they need to stop devaluing us. They need to stop the war on our identities, and stop staying silent when their friends make sexist jokes or comments about women in the studio. A lot of women tell me about this, and it's devastating. We need true allies. We need men who empower women and queer people, who hire and work with us, and who don't ask us for lower rates or extra services.

On our side, as women and non-binary people, we need to organise: create organisations, studios, groups that talk about this and that empower women and queer people to make music, record, produce, mix and master. We also need to stop devaluing ourselves. I kind of hate the victim mindset right now. I want us to take our share of responsibility. We're definitely bullied, but we need to get stronger collectively to resist it.

Most women in the field are cis and straight, and many learned sound with their boyfriends. That's fine, but as queer women we can't rely on men, and I hope those women will at some point ally with us to build the infrastructure we need to keep existing and growing in this industry. We need to stop being so male-centred and start thinking about our interests as a class. At the same time, there are still gender, race and class issues within that class, and we need to address them so nobody is left out. Women can be hard on each other too, and that needs to soften. Most women still choose to work with men, and most of those men don't empower us, the queer and women engineers.

Overall, we need to start paying women and queer people the same as men. Right now we're not treated as equals. We're always asked for lower rates, even though the path to get where we are is harder for us. Being a trans woman doesn't make my work any less good than a man's. We should have the right to be listened to, to be valued, to be considered for a big project, whatever our identity.

When I had a studio, I almost always had an intern. I got a lot of requests, and I always said yes to women and queer people. I've also run workshops for women and queer people to learn studio work, and we did the Sous-mixées conference. And I end up working almost exclusively with women and queer people. There are so many studios run by men that the women and queer people who need a place where they feel safe to express themselves, learn and work on audio tend to come to us.

### What advice would you give a woman or a queer person who wants to become a sound engineer, on a small budget?

Just do it! Don't wait for anyone's validation, especially men's. You can take up all the space you want. It's a lot of work, a lot of time spent on things most people don't care about, but at the end of the day there are a lot of women and queer artists in this industry who desperately need people like us when it's time to produce their record.

You don't need much to start: a good pair of headphones (like Audeze), an audio interface and a mic will get you to a good starting point. Do your best, research what you don't understand, practise a lot, and you'll get there.

Also, please know that every woman and queer artist I welcomed in my studio had impostor syndrome, me included. You're not alone, we deal with this as a class. I've read many stories of men in this industry who were asked to mix a record while they were interns: they didn't know how, they did it anyway, and sometimes it launched their careers. We need to start building those opportunities for women and queer people too.

## Today

### What are you working on right now?

I'm finishing my new album, *TRAPPED IN THIS WRLD*, which comes out in 2027. I come from shoegaze, but I turned to rap for a year: I had a lot to say about what happened to me. I'm putting the last touches on the mastering right now.

I'm also preparing a tour in early November with this new solo project. It will be my first solo tour. I've been touring since 2011, but always in rock bands. I'm happy I changed everything. I love the music I'm making right now, and I have so much freedom: I can switch between all my genres, ambient, trap, pop, and change my setlist depending on how I feel. I also feel freer not having to play guitar and sing at the same time. That used to take all my energy.

I've also started experimenting more with production. It's really nice to just play instruments for artists, it's a lot of fun. I'm trying to collaborate more too. Even if I can do everything on my own, when I combine my energy with another talented producer or musician it can be really explosive, and it sounds different, fresher. A few weeks ago, with my friend Plus Plus Eden, we produced a techno track for an Italian singer. We made a beat, he played some synths and I played bass while Angèle wrote her vocals. We ended up with a really interesting track, all in one day.

## Anotyne's plugins, second-hand

Here is what the developers say about reselling the plugins she mentions:

- **[FabFilter Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4)**: transferable. The seller buys a License Transfer (about €10) in their FabFilter account and moves the license to the buyer. [FabFilter transfer rules](/developers/fabfilter).
- **[Tone Projects Uni-L](/developers/tone-projects/uni-l-master-limiter)**: transferable through Tone Projects' License Transfer page, $35 per license, paid by the seller. [Tone Projects transfer rules](/developers/tone-projects).
- **Arturia TAPE-201**: Arturia allows transfers. [Arturia transfer rules](/developers/arturia).
- **Softube Weiss Deess**: transferred through iLok License Manager ($25 iLok fee per license or bundle, max $50 per order). [Softube transfer rules](/developers/softube).
- **UAD Manley Massive Passive and Ampex ATR-102**: Universal Audio doesn't allow its plugins to be resold on their own. [Universal Audio transfer rules](/developers/universal-audio).
- **Schwabe Digital HiFAL**: Schwabe Digital doesn't allow transfers. [Schwabe Digital transfer rules](/developers/schwabe-digital).

TAPE-201 and Weiss Deess aren't in our list yet. Both developers are, so when you sell, you can add them yourself from the [Sell form](/sell). And if you're looking for one, search for it on [Browse](/browse) and set an alert: we'll email you as soon as someone lists it.
