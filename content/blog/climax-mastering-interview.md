Jean-Charles Panizza has been mastering for over fifteen years. He started Climax Mastering in 2010 with a friend from sound school and has run it on his own since 2020, in an all-wood room in Bazemont, west of Paris, built around a mostly analog chain and a few carefully chosen plugins.

We talked about how he found his way into mastering, why hardware still runs his chain, the three plugins he would keep, mastering for vinyl, and the new room he is building.

## Career

### Could you start by introducing yourself, and tell us how you found your way into mastering?

I'm Jean-Charles Panizza, I'm 42, and I've been mastering for over fifteen years. I'm passionate about music and sound, and that started very early. I took percussion and drum lessons as a kid, and I formed my first rock bands around 15. Things really took off in my early twenties when I started playing bass and went on my first tours, while I was studying musicology on the side. I set up my first studio around 22, when I started studying sound engineering. I was recording and mixing with whatever I could get my hands on. Fun fact: I recorded my band's first album with a bunch of SM57s and a PreSonus interface borrowed from a friend. We didn't have enough mic stands, so we used whatever was lying around... and honestly, it didn't turn out that bad!

My first real taste of mastering came when I needed to release some tracks for one of my projects and had zero budget to hire a pro. It took me quite a few attempts before I got something "decent". Later, when I worked on projects with a bit more money behind them, we'd send our tracks to professional mastering engineers, and I'd practice trying to get close to their results. After countless hours turning the knobs on my cracked plugins, I started to understand what mastering means to me today: understanding the song, and making it shine.

I won't lie, I love the technical side. I'm a total geek! But what I love most is music.

### How did Climax Mastering come to life, and how has the studio changed over the years?

I started Climax Mastering in 2010 with a friend from sound school. Since 2020, I've been running the studio on my own. It hasn't moved, but the setup has changed a lot!

When you share a room with another engineer, you have to make compromises so it works for everyone. Now I have my own setup, and I've fine-tuned my workflow until it fits me perfectly. I got rid of or changed everything that didn't work for me, and kept everything I loved about the original studio. It's become something that really feels like mine, almost tailor-made. And it keeps evolving: every piece in my chain matters, and every new piece of gear, hardware or software, has to earn its place.

## Workflow

### Your mastering chain is mostly analog. What does hardware bring that plugins can't quite match?

First, I'd say color. The whole chain was built with that in mind, from the choice of units to the order they sit in. Any unit I'm not using on a track can be taken completely out of the chain with the push of a button, so A/B comparisons are smoother and decisions feel more intuitive. I can adjust gain staging at several points in the chain for the units that are sensitive to it, and get exactly the color I'm after. To make all that possible, I designed and built a box myself that's halfway between a switcher and a mastering console. It lets me work with hardware as fluidly as I would with plugins.

Second, workflow. I know this chain inside out, and that lets me get to what I want to hear in an intuitive way.

Maybe you could get similar results working in the box... Personally, I get better results this way.

### That said, which digital tools have earned a permanent place in your workflow?

Some digital tools are just too good to ignore, even for an analog geek like me! The ones I reach for most are limiters, de-essers, some very precise EQs, and of course restoration tools. What I find most interesting about them is that they offer solutions that simply don't exist in the analog world.

I really can't choose between analog and digital. I think they complement each other really well!

### How does your approach change when you master for vinyl compared to streaming?

Honestly, my approach doesn't really change. Of course there are some technical differences between formats, and I adapt my masters to where they're going. When there are several formats, I deliver several versions. But what stays the same is what matters most to me: making the music shine. That's my approach!

For vinyl releases, I deliver a dedicated vinyl premaster that helps it translate better on that format. And when the overall cutting level isn't an issue, I usually leave a bit more dynamics. It's not necessarily a limitation of the format. In my experience, vinyl listeners tend to care about dynamics. Like me!

## Plugins

### If you had to keep only three plugins, which ones would they be?

My favorite and most-used limiter is [Softube's Weiss DS1](/developers/softube/weiss-ds1-mk3). It usually works really well in my chain, and its hidden gem is the frequency-dependent compression.

Second would be the de-esser from the same Softube Weiss series, [Weiss Deess](https://www.softube.com/deess). It's super transparent, has two bands, and is very tweakable. I use it for way more than just de-essing!

Third is one of the newest additions to my chain: [Relab's emulation of the Maselec MLA-4](https://relabdevelopment.com/mla-4/). I don't own the hardware, so I can't really say how faithful it is, but it's earned a regular spot in my chain, especially for its expander features.

### Most of us have plugins we bought and barely use. Are there any sleeping on your hard drive?

Of course! There are quite a few plugins I've bought and rarely or never use. I tend to try a lot of plugins when they come out... and if I like one, I buy it. Sometimes a tool is excellent but just doesn't find its place in my chain.

I don't like having "too many" options, so I tend to hide the ones I rarely use. But I know they're there, and every now and then I'll dig one back up!

### Have you ever bought or sold used gear or plugin licenses?

Used hardware: yes, all the time. A good chunk of my gear was bought second-hand, and I've sold quite a bit too. Apart from the occasional damage from shipping companies, it's generally gone well. And when you hand gear over in person, you sometimes end up meeting really great people.

I'd never thought about buying or selling plugin licenses, but now that I know it's possible, I don't see any reason not to!

## Today

### Your new studio is on its way. What do you have in mind for it?

I don't want to give too much away, but yes, I've got the slightly crazy plan of building a new mastering room. It'll have a lot in common with my current studio: all wood, a big bright room with a nice high ceiling. I'm paying particular attention to the materials and building techniques, for environmental, aesthetic and, of course, sonic reasons. Right now I'm lucky enough to work in a great room that sounds fantastic. But when you're passionate about what you do, is that really a reason not to aim for something even better?

### What advice would you give to a young engineer who wants to get into mastering?

Listen to a lot of music! When you master every day, you deal with every genre imaginable. You need a broad musical culture and an open mind. There's fascinating music in every style!

Technique and gear are just tools. You have to practice and put in the work to master them, and that's not optional. Same goes for your ears and your listening.

But what will really make the difference is your musical culture, your ears and your sensitivity. And, quite simply, your passion ;)
