Globe Audio Mastering has been mastering records in Bordeaux since 1999. Alexis Bardinet founded it with Bertrand Reboulleau, and Lorenzo Bassaletti joined the studio in 2021. Their credits run from SCH, Bigflo & Oli and Noir Désir to Ibrahim Maalouf and Katia & Marielle Labèque, and most of their work is still done in the room with the artist, on a chain of 15 to 20 machines, four Studer tape recorders among them.

We talked about how each of them got into mastering, what has changed since the days of DAT tapes and CD burning, how two engineers share one studio, custom-modified gear, loudness in the streaming era, what makes a mix easy or hard to master, and the few plugins they keep next to the analog chain.

## Career

### Alexis, Globe started in 1999. How did you get into mastering, and how did Globe begin?

**Alexis:** As the son of a musician, I was immersed in the music world quite young. I learned the drums (at the Lajudie school) and a bit of piano, and when my parents opened rehearsal studios in Bordeaux, I quickly found myself recording the bands that came through. Then I was lucky enough to take sound lessons with Paul de Jenlis, who was a real mentor to me. He is the one who showed me it was possible to be a sound engineer and make a living from it. After that, I went back to Paris for the Novocom sound engineering school, a three-year course. That's where I met my business partner Bertrand Reboulleau, as well as a great many people we still work with in the industry, whether students or teachers.

Then I landed an internship at the Translab mastering studio in Paris, where I ended up being hired as an assistant. After that, I also worked as a mastering assistant at Universal Music. It's quite funny, because at Translab we worked on SADiE (Windows) and at Universal on Sonic (Mac), but that didn't stop me from quickly moving to another system when we created Globe Audio. It's the system we still use today, because we were among its beta testers: Pyramix, by the Swiss company Merging Technologies.

### Lorenzo, you joined Globe in 2021. What was your path before, and how did you end up working with Alexis?

**Lorenzo:** I started by studying at the EICAR school in Paris, which got me several internships in studios in the capital. My first one was at Studio Grande Armée and the second at Sodasound. Then, through recording and mixing workshops, I got to work at Studio Caverne. That's how I started getting my first sessions as an engineer and assistant. Around that time, I had also teamed up with two friends from school to set up a studio of our own, Mountain Records. We did everything: recording, mixing, mastering, editing for film. The manager of Caverne, Nicolas Sacco, then put me in touch with Twin Studio Paris, where he had worked for several years. There too, I got to prove myself as an assistant and control-room engineer.

My arrival at Globe Audio came after a period of questioning around the middle or end of Covid. I wanted to open up to new horizons, leave Paris to see what opportunities I could find elsewhere, and get closer to the ocean, which had always been the setting I dreamed of living in. So I followed the classic formula and applied to just about every studio on the French coast, with an interesting cluster around Bordeaux and Toulouse. A year later I met Alexis and did a two-week observation internship through Pôle Emploi (the French employment agency). We got along right away and shared the same vision. That's how I was hired as an assistant, and then started getting my own sessions. It's now been six years since I arrived at Globe Audio.

### Over more than 25 years, what has changed the most in mastering, and what hasn't changed at all?

**Alexis:** Over the last 25 years, an enormous amount has changed. In our early work, we were on Sound Forge, with analog tapes or DAT, and we burned CDs. What changed a lot is that back then, when artists arrived here with their DATs or tapes, they often hadn't listened to their mix since the studio, because they had no player, so it was several weeks or months after the end of the mixing. That meant they were rediscovering the songs, sometimes with good surprises, sometimes less good ones. Another funny thing was that we did the choice of versions. On the DATs, there were often two or three different mixes. Sometimes the differences were written down, but not always. In that case, you had to listen and try to detect which one was the best version. That's a huge difference from the work today, where nearly all the artists we welcome here have audio software at home and know how to use it. Whether they are artists or mixers, they already have a fairly precise idea of the final result.

What doesn't change, for us who work mainly in person and in analog, is having a different band every morning, from a different musical style, from different regions or countries. That means very varied ways of making and listening to music.

## Two engineers, one studio

### How do you split the work between you? Do you each have your own clients and genres, or do you sometimes work on the same record?

**Alexis:** We split it fairly randomly, even if we each have our preferred days in the control room. It can also depend on each one's availability during the week, and on musical sensibilities (no split by genre, each of us is able and willing to do everything!).

**Lorenzo:** Since I arrived, I mainly take care of new clients, because Alexis already has a well-filled schedule with the artists who are used to working with him. It's very rare, but it has happened that we both work on the same project, when it is fragmented or when the tracks come in one by one over several weeks.

### Do you hear things differently? Any example of a master where you disagreed, and how you settled it?

**Lorenzo:** It's hard to say whether we hear things differently. I think so, because it's human: we don't have the same culture, even if it is fairly close, at least in terms of musical taste. But there are bound to be things that come back and others that differ. There isn't really a master where we disagree, precisely because we communicate a lot beforehand about the work. Then we each let the other carry his vision through to the end, without interfering with the other's work. But when one of us needs an opinion, we sometimes ask the other for an extra pair of ears to be sure.

**Alexis:** That's why we get along well: we both hear the same things, even with different cultures! Lorenzo is Franco-Chilean, with his deep culture of Latin American music. And me with my jazz-musician dad, who gave me a culture of jazz and classical. We're quite complementary!

## The studio

### What's your setup today (room, monitors, converters, analog outboard)? Some of your gear is custom-built: what, and why?

**Alexis:** Our exact setup is on the [Globe Audio website](https://www.globe-audio.com/studio)! For a few years now, we've been working with Genève Musique (Ben & Guigz) in Switzerland, who maintain our tape machines. They come for about a week a year and take care of our four Studers. They also handle everything that is a bit custom here: small electronic modifications, for instance in the choice and matching of tubes, and so on. They source the rare parts for us and let us choose them.

As for customised machines, on the Lavry Gold for example, they built an oversized power supply modification. We also have a Neumann W495STB vinyl-cutting EQ, the white version, which is stereo and has Telefunken V72 transformers: a machine with an extraordinary colour. And we got hold of the Manley Pultecs that used to be in George Michael's studio in London.

They are currently working on a modification of our Tube-Tech HLT 2AM, which we love...

## Workflow

### Walk us through a typical master: what do you listen for first, and what does your usual chain look like?

**Lorenzo:** First, I do a first listen of the whole song, from start to finish. That lets me work out several points:

- what drives the song
- what its important elements are
- whether there's a vocal that stands out, or whether it is maybe a bit less important to the song
- whether the energy is smooth, aggressive, slow

All of that gives me a good picture of what's going on in the song, so I can draw up a technical work plan, guided by a sensitive artistic vision. In other words, my goal is to underline the elements that seem to me to bring the most emotion to the song and the most interaction with the listener.

As for my usual chain, it often starts with one or two rather coloured EQs, to make the more radical decisions right at the start. Then I refine that vision as I go, with more surgical EQs, and with multiband compressors, which let me work a bit more precisely on each band. Then I go through an output compressor, which brings gain, light saturation and more density to the song. And finally I hit the converter stages, to clip or limit the song. Within that chain, we can bring in lots of different, more drastic colours, with much more coloured EQs (Pultec, Neumann, tape machine, etc.).

### Your client list goes from SCH and Bigflo & Oli to Noir Désir, Ibrahim Maalouf and Katia & Marielle Labèque. How does your approach change between rap, rock, jazz and classical?

**Lorenzo:** My approach changes with the genre and with what the listener is used to hearing in that style. That doesn't mean I'm going to conform to what the public expects. But if we tell one style from another, it's because there are codes attached to it, so some things are inevitably brought forward more than others. I won't necessarily change my basic protocol, as we discussed in the previous question. What changes is mainly the approach to loudness: whether I make a song louder or quieter, depending on the listening levels the public is used to and the density the song needs, so as not to shock the listener with too different an approach, unless that is the artist's decision.

Because what can also change is what the artist asks for. Without making a big cliché of it, it's true that most of the time, in rap, rock and very contemporary jazz, we're asked to bring colour and density, so to take slightly more forced positions, to hit a bit harder. Whereas in more traditional genres like jazz or classical, we'll do more surgical things, a bit more respectful of the source material. In those cases, our approach can be different.

**Alexis:** What changes a lot, beyond the way of working, is above all the communication we have with the artists, the psychological relationship. The essential thing is to handle their arrival well, and quickly try to offer them something they like. My technique is not to ask them for instructions. I do my settings and let them listen. And it's true that almost always, we get the validation at that moment. Because sometimes, in their intentions, they would have told me to go black when I saw white. But in the end, when we play it to them, it's what suits them, and I think that's something that works well here. That's where I think our vision is right, and to me that's the most important thing. But you have to accept one thing: if at the start, in the morning, for whatever reason, it doesn't exactly match expectations, you can very quickly be less focused and manage that first part of the day less well.

Whereas if from the start we reassure the artist, listen to them and do something they like, everyone in the control room is having a great time, the sound engineer signs off, and the rest of the day runs by itself. In the end, that step matters more than the change of style. Adapting to the style is something we know how to do.

### Loudness: how do you deal with clients who want it louder, now that streaming normalises everything?

**Lorenzo:** Streaming platforms do normalise every song today. Nevertheless, the question of loudness remains important, because that level also determines the density of a song, its perceived level and its energy. Not to mention its adaptability to different listening situations, in very different places: noisy environments, not always of good quality, or rather poor acoustic conditions. It's something that does affect the intelligibility of the song. So we don't follow the -14 LUFS "standard", since that figure is tied to the platforms' normalisation, for the public's listening comfort. It shouldn't be taken into account for the final master file. So yes, you need to reach relatively high LUFS levels (again, it depends on the style), but it has to be said that this loudness level is still quite powerful today. And so, depending on the artistic requirements and the requests (which can be very different), we can end up with fairly dynamic levels, or with louder levels and much more intensity in extreme cases.

### What makes a mix easy (or hard) to master? One tip for people sending you their mixes?

**Lorenzo:** A mix that is too heavy in the upper mids and highs, without enough control (hi-hats, vocal sibilance, etc.), will be a bit harder to master, because we'll need to control that frequency band and at the same time add some to bring more brightness, detail, intelligibility and adaptability to different playback systems. When we have that problem, it does slow us down a bit. Then there's dynamics. A mix can be too compressed and limited (which is not necessarily a bad thing). But if it's very drastic, it means the person isn't leaving us enough room to offer them more drastic things. We no longer have room in the dynamics to do something contrasted, with colour. Conversely, if the project is too dynamic, with far too much separation, it can be complicated to keep impact and a good hold on the dynamics while being loud and dense. Overall, a mix that is easier to master is a mix that is under control, where everything is in its place, whether it's the relationship in the low end between the bass instruments, a good midrange, big highs. A good mix makes a good master. And if you need it, there's a technical guide on the Globe Audio website with lots of tips: [GAM mastering guide (PDF, in French)](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5ef09e9f24a1314748ee021a/t/603ccf4e3d06494e3af1e516/1614597978284/GAM+guide+mastering+FR.pdf).

**Alexis:** In the past, the feedback we gave was about files that were too limited or too compressed. Now it's sometimes the opposite: things with too much dynamic range, where people haven't framed and held things enough. What we often tell artists is to bring the necessary glue anyway. That can be done with a plugin compressor, a bus compressor, a 33609 or an SSL. On average, we get files with an RMS around -18 dBFS and peaks around -3 to -6. But there is no absolute rule! It depends on the style, the mixer, the equipment, there are so many parameters... What we often get are mixes that are a bit mono, lacking openness, lacking articulation between kick and bass, often a bit confused. Often some confusion in the low mids, and often upper mids that are a bit aggressive, a bit hard. We no longer give feedback on sibilance, because we know how to control it with all the tools we have. The good rule is to have a mix that is a bit duller, without being completely muffled, but not too bright and aggressive. Small things that, in the end, matter enormously...

## Plugins

### What are your must-have plugins?

**Lorenzo:** It's true that we use very few plugins here. We're more attached to doing the work in analog, because we like doing it that way, it's our vision, and quite simply we enjoy it more. But we do use a few plugins that help complete our analog arsenal. There's [Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4): a great classic, but indispensable when you want to make very surgical little touch-ups if needed. I don't think there's anything better. Then there's the [Ozone](/developers/izotope) limiter. I use it when there's a need for a tiny bit more level, limiting, control and loudness. It's a very good tool, does the job well and is very versatile. And one last one, the Sonnox [Oxford Limiter](/developers/sonnox/oxford-limiter). I prefer it to the Sonnox Saturator because it has the "Enhance" function, which adds a bit more pep to an already finished master, more forwardness, a fairly light extra harmonic saturation, and gives a bit more edge to the transients, on the drums, a bit more overall punch.

**Alexis:** We do use plugins, even though it's true that for me, most of the albums I've done over 27 years were done on hardware, with an often fairly heavy chain (between 15 and 20 machines). Mind you, that chain also includes digital machines, in particular all the Weiss tools (we own nearly the whole range), as well as the M6000: I love the programs in it. Back to plugins: I'm quite a fan of Sonnox. I do like their limiter, it's something I use quite a bit. I like the [Inflator](/developers/sonnox/oxford-inflator) too, I use it from time to time. And for EQ, Pro-Q as well, which is terrific as a dynamic EQ for post-mastering refinement. It has to be said that for dealing with unwanted noises (clicks, buzz, hum, crackle), the iZotope tools are exceptional... even if I do sometimes like to remove clicks by redrawing them in Pyramix!

### Where does analog still win for you, and where have plugins taken over?

**Lorenzo:** It's quite hard to answer that question, because again, we work mainly in analog, but plugins remain indispensable to us. For me, neither is better than the other. Both have advantages and drawbacks, it all depends on your working habits. Analog gets you drastic, committed colours quickly. There's an intuitive side, thanks to the absence of visuals, that lets us focus on what we hear and feel: we keep the essential and dwell less on details. But plugins bring fine, surgical things and give us more handling on the material we work with. Also, with plugins you can get distortions, but they are easy to correct (unlike analog, which brings clipping or harmonic saturation effects linked to the use of tubes).

**Alexis:** For me it's a whole. Not only the analog, but the Globe Audio setup, with digital hardware too, the Weiss units and the engines of the M6000... I've been in this control room with these machines around me for so long that this is where I'm intuitive, fast, creative. Of course I had to learn plugins and how to use them, and I do use them. I find them less creative, less fun, less committed. I feel like I'm in something a bit more analytical, less in direct contact with the sound and the music. So it's also a matter of ways of doing things, of working, and because I learned to work on tape and analog consoles. That's how I learned my trade. For me, that's where the difference lies. That's where I'm far more effective with analog, to immediately get the result I like.

That said, plugins do serve us for very fine things: for example, we sometimes get stems that need readjusting with a bit of delay, a bit of reverb. One incredible thing for me is iZotope's [RX](/developers/izotope), that one is unbeatable. I find it fabulous, it lets you remove distortions, mouth noises, all the saturations, clean up the tracks before running them through the analog. There, for me, is real added value, and there's no analog equivalent. So for cleaning tools, everything that is really precision work, surgery: yes to plugins. But again, for creativity, instinct, musicality, I feel better in the analog world.

## Today

### A recent project you're particularly proud of, and why?

**Lorenzo:** This one is more or less recent, as it's two years old, but I'll mention the album "Ultra Sensible" by the rapper A2H. I find it particularly successful, whether in the overall teamwork, the composition, the arrangement, the technique... It's clear in its artistic direction, with good choices (in the mixes we were delivered, for example). Draedfull (artistic direction, arrangements) and Anh-Ninh (mixes) did an incredible job, which really allowed me to take their vision even further and bring off a project I find particularly well controlled. The songs are great and varied, and they listen well on lots of different systems and in lots of contexts. I can always find what we did in the mix and the master again, which lets me enjoy it all the more!

**Alexis:** For my part, the project I'm particularly happy with is the latest project by Léon Phal, "Bodies and Ideas", which was recorded at Daft Music Studios in Belgium and mixed by Jordan Kouby at QDS Studios. I had already done a record with him about ten years ago. Musically, it's exceptional. Jordan's work is truly royal, for me, to work with. It's a quality of mix that allows great creativity on the record. And it's true that it's a record that came out a few days ago, and I'm seeing a great reception from the press, and from the feedback we've had on it. That's something that is really a pleasure, and it's also what I strongly believe in: a beautiful record starts with good compositions and good musicians, and that leads to a good recording. If that went well, you make a good mix. And if there's a good mix, the mastering is the cherry on the cake, the little finishing step. Those are really the projects where I feel I was able to add my small stone to the building.

### One piece of advice for a young engineer who wants to get into mastering today?

**Lorenzo:** Here are a few pieces of advice I would give: have a minimum of experience in recording and mixing (and why not live sound too), to understand the problems, stakes, limits and conditions of everything that happens before and after mastering. Being aware and clear-headed about all those points will let you make better decisions about the material you're going to work with. Stay empathetic and understanding towards clients, and don't underestimate the importance of everything that may have happened before the songs reach us.

**Alexis:** To young people, I would say above all: be curious. And that's one of the advantages of today's generations. With access to streaming platforms, you can immediately listen to any style of music from different countries. The amount of music out there is infinite. To me, that's a chance. It's something we didn't have before. I remember buying my CD once a month. Sometimes I didn't like it, or only liked one song. But since I had bought it, I listened to it, and listened to it again, until I started to appreciate it!!! Now there's this infinity of possibilities. You can get lost in it, but it's still a chance. There's that extraordinary tool, YouTube, to watch tutorials, studio tours, ways of doing things, to learn about engineers, about history... I also love reading everything about the history of studios (for example "Recording the Beatles"), and watching documentaries (for example "Sound City", which is really interesting on the history of studios). All these ways of finding out how things were done in the past, it's fascinating!

## Globe Audio's plugins, second-hand

Alexis and Lorenzo keep very few plugins next to their analog chain, and all of them can be resold. Here is what the developers say:

- **[FabFilter Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4)**: about €10 for a License Transfer, bought by the seller in their FabFilter account, then the seller moves the license to the buyer's full name and email. Educational and NFR licenses can't be transferred, and bundles only move as a whole. FabFilter treats each major version as a separate product, so an older [Pro-Q 3](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-3) can be sold on its own. [FabFilter transfer rules](/developers/fabfilter).
- **iZotope [Ozone 12](/developers/izotope/ozone-12-advanced) and [RX 12](/developers/izotope/rx-12-advanced)**: free transfer (plus the $25 iLok fee if the product was activated with iLok), requested from iZotope Account Support by the original owner. Only full, purchased licenses: time-limited, educational, NFR and hardware-bundled licenses aren't eligible, and a product can only be transferred once. [iZotope transfer rules](/developers/izotope).
- **Sonnox [Oxford Limiter](/developers/sonnox/oxford-limiter) and [Oxford Inflator](/developers/sonnox/oxford-inflator)**: no Sonnox fee, only the iLok transfer fee (capped at $50 when several licences go to the same account at once), paid by the seller, who moves the licence to the buyer's iLok User ID in iLok License Manager; Sonnox support then sends the buyer re-registration instructions. Full licences only, at least 90 days old, no NFR. Mind the UAD versions of the same plugins: those only move together with the Universal Audio hardware they are registered to. [Sonnox transfer rules](/developers/sonnox).

Looking for one of them? Search for it on [Browse](/browse) and set an alert: we'll email you as soon as someone lists it. And if you have licenses you never use, you can list them for free on the [Sell page](/sell).
