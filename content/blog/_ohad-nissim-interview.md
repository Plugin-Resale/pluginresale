Ohad Nissim is a Grammy-nominated mastering engineer based in Luxembourg. He has mastered records for Chris Brown, Ciara, XG, Polo G, Ava Max, Lucky Daye and Big Sean, from a home studio built around an analog chain he has spent three years refining. With Teezio and Bainz, he also runs RTM Audio, a plugin company made by working engineers for working engineers.

We talked about his road from club DJ to mastering, the person who changed everything, his chain, the left/right EQ trick he uses on every master, loudness, gain staging, and why staying out of the way is the best signature.

## Career

### How did you get into music, and how did you end up specialising in mastering?

From a young age I was chasing new releases, trying to figure out what the next hit would be, especially in commercial club music. Then I started organizing parties and eventually DJing.

Later on, I moved to London and found a job tuning club systems (setting sub levels and that kind of stuff). On my second round in London, I did a year at SAE, basically living in the studios there: recording, mixing, and trying to fill my brain with everything related to the engineering world. I expected this to open doors for me, but it didn't, and I ended up moving to Berlin, trying to get back into the DJ world and music production. Running out of money, I got sucked into a corporate job for three years, until I decided to go all in on engineering.

I signed up to SoundBetter when the website was just starting, at first offering mixing, until someone asked me if I could master. I'd learned early on that you never say no, so I gave it a go, and the client loved it. From there, I shared every single credit I collected and grew from that. At first I did both mixing and mastering, until mastering alone was enough to keep me moving forward, and that's what I decided to focus on.

### You're Grammy-nominated and have mastered for Chris Brown, Ciara, XG, Polo G, Ava Max and many more. Is there a project or a moment that changed things for you?

More than a moment, it was a person. Hooking up with Teezio was the thing that opened the road for me and gave me the validation that I can do this at a high level. Teezio's support, and him introducing me to people in the industry, really gave me the tailwind I needed, in combination with my own ambitions and dreams.

In terms of one specific project, the FLO album was probably a big push forward. It was the first real full album I did start to finish at this level, with everything I brought to the table back then, and of course it ended up being nominated for a Grammy.

### Where are you based today, and what does your studio look like?

Currently I'm based in Luxembourg, a very small and quiet country, which gives me the headspace I need while still being very well situated for travelling.

My studio is in my house, which makes things easier in terms of schedule. Working with the US, I sometimes need to work late, get up early, or deliver files at lightning speed, especially earlier in my US market days, when you want to impress.

In terms of monitors, I've got two pairs. The main ones in the studio are the LIBRIO towers, which aren't fully on the market yet. In the living room I've got Dutch & Dutch 8c's, where I test "casual listening" on the things I master.

In terms of the mastering process itself, I'm still big on analog gear. I've spent the last three years perfecting my chain, and my system just sounds so good it's irreplaceable. I do love plugins though, and recently, together with Teezio and Bainz, we started building our own plugins under RTM Audio. These plugins are mostly what occupies my plugin chain after the analog gear. I won't go into what's better, because it's all tools and they are what you make of them. It's just great to have that flexibility.

## Workflow

### Walk us through a typical master: what do you listen for first, and what does your chain usually look like?

My main goal nowadays is to stay as close as possible to the mix, but make it sound better. I'll usually listen to where the vocals sit, the amount of sub and how it affects the muddiness of the mix, the punch of the kick if there is one, and lastly, whether there's anything harsh in the mix that I need to smooth out.

In terms of my chain, the analog side runs: Lavry Quintessence → Knif Eksa → Unfairchild → Terry Audio CEQ → Knif Solmu → Hum Audio LAAL → Lavry Savitr. Then in the box, I use DMASK2, sometimes TXB2, sometimes CXB, sometimes CHILL, [Kirchhoff EQ](/developers/three-body-technology/kirchhoff-eq) if I need to bring back punch, and I finish with a limiter for the last gain push, either PEAK2 or [TDR Limiter 6 GE](/developers/tokyo-dawn-records/limiter-6-ge). (DMASK2, TXB2, CHILL, CXB and PEAK2 are all RTM Audio plugins.)

### You sometimes EQ the left and right channels differently to create a sense of width. Most engineers would reach for mid/side instead. Where did that idea come from, and how do you keep the image balanced and mono-compatible?

I actually use them in combination: I'll use M/S on the Knif Eksa and the EQ trick on the Terry Audio CEQ. The trick actually comes from the CEQ manual, where Francis, Terry Audio's founder and designer, suggests trying it, and I fell in love with it, the organic widening of it. I use it on every single master, it's always there.

Honestly, I just don't overthink it. It just works, and I've never encountered image balance or mono issues, so I keep going. One of our plugins, CXB, was built on this technique, and it sounds so, so good. I can't wait to put it out.

### You work a lot in hip-hop, R&B, pop and K-pop. How does your approach change between genres, and how do you deal with loudness expectations today?

It doesn't. My approach and processing are exactly the same across all genres. What does change is my understanding and knowledge of the genre, which also connects directly to loudness: every genre feels comfortable around a certain level of loudness that you're trying to aim for.

Of course, some clients ask for more, but it's not an issue. I'll explain if we need to compromise anything, usually a bit of sub energy. I also look at loudness more in terms of size, width and thickness rather than numbers. That's where my M/S work and the EQ tricks come in handy: they always make things sound bigger compared to the mix.

### What makes a mix easy (or hard) to master? Any advice for engineers sending you their mixes?

It all starts and ends with proper gain staging and peak control in the mix. I think that's the biggest difference I see between experienced and less experienced mixing engineers. That's why less experienced engineers have a hard time putting out loud or thick mixes: the peaks are eating their headroom, and without proper control throughout the whole mix, you're maxing out or distorting pretty early down the line and start working backwards.

It seems like newer engineers have stopped paying attention to gain staging and are looking for shortcuts. Master the art of gain staging and peak control, it will make your life so much easier.

## Plugins

### What are your must-have plugins, and why?

Some self-promotion here, but not from a selling point of view :) [RTM Audio](https://www.rtmaudio.com/), the plugin company I run with Teezio and Bainz. Or, as we like to present it: working engineers making plugins for working engineers.

Basically, we're taking all of our experience through the years and building tools that will save you so much time when mixing and mastering. It's all about saving time, working faster and getting results, and that's why we're doing this: to help you guys out. We're also keeping it cheap ;)

### Is there a plugin you find overrated, or one you bought and never used?

I've purchased so many plugins through the years, and I'm using almost none of them. Since I don't like to diss anyone or bring down other businesses, I'll just say: just because a plugin is expensive doesn't mean it's better than the $30 one. And always compare honestly, with a level-matched A/B, otherwise loudness will fool you.

## Today

### What are you working on right now?

I just finished Lucky Daye's album, some Big Sean tracks, LNGSHOT in the K-pop world, and I also had to travel to master locally on a massive project I can't talk about :)

### What advice would you give to a young engineer who wants to get into mastering?

Listen to a lot of music, every genre. Pay attention to where the vocals sit, how the groove works, and what's driving the mix forward. Then, from a technical point of view, do whatever you can to stay out of the way. Don't fall into the trap of trying to leave your signature sound or anything like that. If you're able to stay out of the way, that's your signature.
