Jesza started out making beats, recording and mixing for the rap crew POSA more than fifteen years ago. Today he produces and mixes for other artists while running his own project, Jesza: club music at fast BPMs on one side, electronica and ambient on the other, with a strong UK bass influence in his recent releases. His track "Humanity" was remixed by Brodinski, and his edits turn PNL, Kalash Criminel, Krim2gwer or Kalika into something else entirely.

We talked about his path, his room in Montreuil, how he handles low end and loudness for the club, and the plugins he can't do without.

## Career

### How did you get into making music, and into sound engineering?

I discovered music very young, first with drums, then guitar. After playing in a few bands in middle school and high school, I joined the rap crew POSA more than fifteen years ago. I made the beats, recorded the rappers, and did the mixing and mastering. I was immediately fascinated by people like Mike Dean, who inspired me a lot.

So I decided to follow that kind of career as best I could: producing for other artists of every genre, as long as I like it and it speaks to me, and mixing and mastering their tracks. Alongside that, I developed my own project, Jesza, focused on electronic and club music, and also moving between ambient and electronica/trip-hop depending on my mood.

### Your music sits between rap, bass music, club, jungle and dub. How would you describe your sound, and how has it evolved since Club Distortion (2018)?

I'd describe my current sound as a hybrid style of electronic music. I like to move between fast-BPM club music to let loose on the dancefloor (like my first release, *Club Distortion*, which already set out what I love about it) and more electronica or ambient things when I want to unwind. In the last few years, discovering the UK bass scene has influenced me enormously. You can hear it a lot in my recent projects, like the EP *Derrière l'écran*, and in what's coming next: my upcoming EP on the Cane Corso label and the demos I'm finishing.

For me, all these styles are closely linked by their references and the way they're made: sampling, drum machines, synths. By digging into the influences of the artists I listened to, I gradually discovered all the scenes around them. Taking part in and organising a few small raves as a teenager also brought me very close to the club scene and made me want to produce that kind of sound.

### Brodinski remixed "Humanity". How did that come about?

Brodinski and his label Bromance were a huge inspiration while I was learning electronic music, just like Gesaffelstein (Bromance) or Sam Tiba (Pelican Fly). I met him indirectly through the Belgian label Pelican Fly, founded by Richelle and DJ Slow. That label really struck me with its hybrid club sounds, a real breath of fresh air at the time (I'm thinking of Cashmere Cat's *Mirror Maru* EP in 2013). Richelle was actually a mentor to me for a long time, the person who taught me and supported me the most in music, after my best friend Kylian, who helped me pay for my first synths.

DJ Slow knew Louis (Brodinski) very well, and we then crossed paths at two or three nights where we were on the same DJ line-up. We got on really well: Louis is very approachable, and we had a lot of tastes in common. So it was a huge pleasure to ask him to remix "Humanity", which is still one of my favourite tracks I've produced, and to have him say yes!

### You're both a producer and a sound engineer. Do you also mix for other artists? How does the engineering side change the way you produce?

I mix for other artists too, of course. I try not to put up barriers. The only question I ask myself is: does the project speak to me, and am I the right person to help them reach their goals?

Being a producer is a huge asset. Above all, it helps me understand when I *shouldn't* step in, to preserve the artistic signature. That can be anything: leaving breaths, floor noise on a recording, or keeping a slightly dirty bass because that's what gives the track its soul. The other way round, I sometimes catch the intention behind a demo that the artist couldn't finish (for lack of time or gear), and I don't hesitate to suggest adding a bit of production during the mix. Some artists come to me specifically for that double role: they know they can bring a demo and we'll push it much further together before mixing it.

And mixing and mastering technique influences the way I write every day. That's what I love about this job, it never stops evolving. The most important thing I've taken from linking the two worlds is the famous "less is more". You hear it all the time, but you forget it far too quickly when you're producing.

## Workflow

### What's your current setup: DAW, monitors, room? Fully in the box, or some hardware?

I've always worked in Ableton Live (I must have started on version 7 or 8). I've tried pretty much every other DAW, but I stayed with Live for its features, its workflow and, of course, its live side.

Right now I rent a shared studio in Montreuil with other composers and engineers. It belongs to engineer Antoine Gaillet, who has worked on Julien Doré's entire career, among others. He owns the gear in the room: a first-generation Trinnov, a Forssell MADA-2 converter and ATC SCM25A monitors. The studio has plenty of outboard (a RETRO STA-Level, a Chandler Curve Bender, Neve 1081 channels, an API 2500 compressor...), but honestly I almost never use it. I sometimes run a few vocals or basses through the STA-Level, because that gentle colour and ultra-smooth compression is always a pleasure to hear, but otherwise I'm fully in the box. It's more than enough for me!

I use the UAD suite a lot since I have an Apollo Twin (mostly the native versions since they started releasing them), FabFilter's Pro-Q (from v2 all the way to v4 today), the Soundtoys suite, and a few things from Plugin Alliance. My favourites there are the Lindell EQ825, the SPL Machine Head and Hum Audio's LAAAL (for the nerds).

That said, hardware still matters for monitoring. The Trinnov + ATC combo radically changed how I see mixing. Moving from my untreated home studio to this setup made my productions take a huge leap. It's not mandatory, but it speeds up decisions a lot, because you can trust your exports.

I also want to give a big shout-out to Steven Slate and his VSX headphones. They've helped me a lot and still do every day to improve my mixes, especially when I'm travelling or working remotely. It's still a very underrated tool: the team did a great job and keeps improving the hardware and the software.

### How do you usually start a track: drums, a sample, sound design, a vocal?

Honestly, there's no right answer to this one! For my own tracks, I go with the feeling: it can be discovering a sample, wanting to make a drum loop, or just jamming on my synths until I find a loop or a melody that grabs me and that I develop.

When I write for other artists, the approach is different. I often ask them for reference tracks that inspire them, and together we decide on the very first element to build the track on.

### You've done edits and remixes of PNL, Kalika, Krim2gwer and more. How do you approach an edit or a remix? Do you work from stems or from the full track?

Making edits started when I began DJing and got closer to the electronic scene. At first, it was the ideal way to mix genres I liked that didn't necessarily meet at the time, or to adapt tracks I had on repeat for the club. Then it became a real exercise that taught me a lot about composition and arrangement.

Deep down, it's a way of expressing my influences through unexpected hybrids. The best way to understand is to go and listen to my edits on SoundCloud or Bandcamp: there's Kalash Criminel as a boosted club version, a Krim2gwer track (normally hyperpop) turned into an electronica ballad, or a Kalika rock song that I heard as trap in my head... I do it above all for fun.

When I started, I took the full track and changed the tonal balance with EQ. But I have to admit that since AI stem separators arrived, it's pure joy. Being able to isolate an a cappella, a drum loop or a melody and work directly on it speeds up the workflow and opens up endless possibilities.

### Club music has to hit hard on a big sound system. How do you handle the low end and loudness? Do you master your own tracks?

For the low end, working on the Trinnov and the ATCs (and the VSX headphones too) radically helped me understand the relationship between kick, bass and sub. Above all, it made me realise that a solid low end is decided long before the mix: it's all in the arrangement and in choosing sounds carefully.

Today I master almost all my tracks myself. It's become part of my workflow: I produce and mix "top-down", very much inspired by the masterclasses of Jaycen Joshua, who happens to be one of the low-end experts. The only masters I've left to someone else were by Alexis Bardinet, for my EP *Humanity* and my single *Journey*. That was for the experience and because I admire his work. At the time I didn't have the technique I have now, and he did a great job.

Mastering myself comes down to several things: I love the exercise, sometimes it was a question of budget, or of not knowing who to trust with my music. I admit I took part in the "loudness war" for a long time, but that's part of learning! Making mistakes teaches you, as long as you notice them. My club masters today are still loud: the goal is for the kick, snare and bass to hit hard enough to move necks. But for every other style, unless the client specifically asks for it, I no longer chase volume at all costs. I let the flow and the constraints of the arrangement or the writing guide me.

## Plugins

### What are your must-have plugins, and why?

Super hard question as always, haha, but here are the ones that come to mind right away:

- **Ableton's stock plugins**: I'm cheating, but they're by far the ones I use the most! EQ Eight, the Racks that allow incredible combinations, the new limiters and saturators, Drum Buss... But the absolute number one: Utility :)
- **FabFilter Pro-Q 4**: one of the best EQs. Extremely versatile, with innovative features that really lighten the workflow and keep up with how music genres evolve.
- **Soundtoys Effect Rack**: the ultimate cheat code, because it brings all the Soundtoys together. I use it a lot when I know I'm going to combine effects (like Decapitator and EchoBoy). It's fast and always gives impressive results.
- **Spectrasonics Omnisphere, the FX part**: my little gatekeep, haha, I never see anyone use it! I use it exactly like the Effect Rack, but it has EVEN more premium built-in effects. It lets me move really fast on vocals without pulling out six UAD plugins. And it's not that heavy on CPU!
- **Knock Clipper**: an underrated clipper for me. It does a great job on drum groups. Its control lets you move easily between soft and hard clipping without looking at the waveform, which forces me to work with my ears rather than my eyes. Very stable, cheap, and I use it very often.

### Is there a plugin you find overrated, or one you bought and never used?

Tough one. I find it hard to trash products, and I usually only buy when I'm sure I'll like it. But the last time I thought "damn, I should have kept my money" was for the Safari Pedals suite. I know it's quite popular, but even though I tested them and gave them a chance, I never use them. I find they have a "sound" that doesn't suit me, and the workflow isn't always well thought out. Luckily I was on a subscription, which I simply cancelled.

### How do you buy plugins: full price, sales, bundles, second-hand? Have you ever resold a license?

I've never resold a license yet, but I've often thought about it, to get some money back on tools I no longer use. For example older versions of some EQs, like Pro-Q 2 or 3, although I'm not sure you can resell them when you're using version 4. The same goes for plugins like Mastering The Mix's FUSER, which I used a lot and then dropped for workflow reasons.

When it comes to buying, I almost always wait for the right moment. UAD and Soundtoys often run sales that let you pick up plugins for far less than the horrible prices announced at launch. Otherwise I rely a lot on demos: I test, and if it becomes essential I buy it, otherwise I uninstall it.

## Today

### What are you working on right now?

Right now I'm fully focused on my Jesza project. I want to grow it, release the tracks I love and play in clubs again. Alongside that, I obviously keep mixing for other artists. Doing both keeps my ears fresh and lets me discover new sounds that inspire my productions.

I'm releasing an EP on the Nantes label Cane Corso by the end of the year, plus a track on one of their compilations, which should come out on vinyl. I'm also working on a lot of demos I'd like to send to labels I love for distribution, and to DJs so they play them in clubs. And I'm always on the lookout for rappers or emerging artists to offer tailor-made beats, or mixing with a really hands-on, close follow-up approach.

### What advice would you give to a young producer making club music in a home studio?

The best advice I can give, looking back today: experiment with everything, test everything, and play in clubs as much as you can. Use it to test your productions on big systems, learn from the mistakes you hear, and too bad if there are some. Sometimes it's exactly that disorienting or unexpected side that surprises the crowd and makes them want to hear the track again. And finally, the timeless *less is more*: for me, a fundamental concept for any artist to take on board.

### Where can people follow you and listen to your work?

You can follow my Instagram, where I try to be as natural as possible, without bending to the industry's rules. I share what I want there, from philosophical and political thoughts to mixing and production tutorials. It's a way to pass things on, the same way I learned in my bedroom, eyes glued to the best YouTube sessions of my idols.

My original productions are on every streaming platform. I also have SoundCloud and Bandcamp, where I put out quite a few exclusives (especially edits that can't be released on Spotify for legal reasons). And I recently opened a Discord server that I'd love to see grow! Same spirit as Instagram, but more personal. The idea is to build a real community where people help each other, trade feedback on demos and share drum kits.

## Jesza's plugins, second-hand

Jesza wonders whether he could resell his older Pro-Q licenses. Here is what the developers say about the plugins he mentions:

- **FabFilter Pro-Q 2, 3 and 4**: FabFilter treats each major version as a separate product (major upgrades are paid), so an older Pro-Q is a license of its own. A transfer costs about €10, bought by the seller in their FabFilter account. If you got Pro-Q 4 as an upgrade from an older version, ask FabFilter whether that older license can still be transferred on its own before listing it. [FabFilter transfer rules](/developers/fabfilter).
- **[Mastering The Mix FUSER](/browse?q=FUSER)**: free to transfer, through Mastering The Mix support. [Mastering The Mix transfer rules](/developers/mastering-the-mix).
- **[Soundtoys Effect Rack](/browse?q=Effect%20Rack)**, **[Decapitator](/browse?q=Decapitator)**, **[EchoBoy](/browse?q=EchoBoy)**: transferred through iLok ($25 fee, paid by the seller), with Soundtoys' consent. [Soundtoys transfer rules](/developers/soundtoys).
- **[Spectrasonics Omnisphere](/browse?q=Omnisphere)**: $50 per instrument, usually paid by the seller. Only the original purchaser can transfer it, once. [Spectrasonics transfer rules](/developers/spectrasonics).
- **[Ableton Live](/browse?q=Live%2012)**: free, self-service transfer from your Ableton account. [Ableton transfer rules](/developers/ableton).
- **UAD plug-ins**: can't be sold on their own, only together with the Apollo or UAD-2 hardware they're registered to. [Universal Audio transfer rules](/developers/universal-audio).
- **Plugin Alliance (Lindell EQ825, SPL Machine Head...)**: self-service transfers are currently unavailable. [Plugin Alliance transfer rules](/developers/plugin-alliance).
