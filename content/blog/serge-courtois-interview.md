Serge Courtois is a mixing engineer working between Los Angeles and Luxembourg. A classically trained pianist turned jazz drummer, he double-majored in drum set performance and music production and engineering at Berklee before moving to LA. His credits include Alec Benjamin's *Narrated for You*, Riton's hit "Friday", Ian Asher's music, and the immersive mixes for the 20th anniversary of Avril Lavigne's *Let Go*. He also makes plugins under the name Mountainroad DSP.

We talked about how a drummer who was always the one DJing ended up mixing, the people he learned from, his first hour on a new song, the projects he is proudest of, the five plugins on every session, the most common mistake he hears in the mixes sent to him, and why every new technique has to beat his proven setup first.

## Career

### How did you get started in music, and when did you know mixing was your path?

I started on classical piano when I was 9. At 14 I got intrigued by percussion and drum set, and played in jazz bands and jazz ensembles at music school. But I was also always the one DJing, with a sound system and a CD player (dating myself here…), and that side of things really fascinated me. I went on to Berklee, where I double-majored in drum set performance and music production and engineering, and then moved to LA.

All through Berklee, mixing was what interested me most. Recording was always fun, but taking a song from a rough shape and molding it into something that sounds great with compression and EQ was what I really enjoyed.

### Who were your mentors, or the key people who shaped your career?

My first boss, Gustavo Borner at Igloo Music, was the first real professional I got to watch at work, and I learned a lot from him. I also learned a great deal indirectly from people like Adam Messinger and Tony Maserati.

### Which project are you most proud of, and why?

Alec Benjamin's music is one. We've worked on a lot of music together over the years, and *Narrated for You* has so many great songs on it that I'm very proud of and that came out beautifully. Lately, Ian Asher's music has been doing so well on social media worldwide that literally everyone I meet has heard one of his songs. That's such a cool feeling, and being involved with it is super fun. Then there are a few obvious ones like "Friday" by Riton, which was a pretty big hit.

Another fun one was the 20th anniversary of Avril Lavigne's *Let Go*, where I did the immersive mixes. That was special to me because I grew up with her music, and you don't usually get to work on the music you grew up with. These were the very songs I was listening to 20 years ago.

## Studio and workflow

### What does your current setup look like: studio, DAW, monitoring?

I work from a home studio. I'm on Dutch & Dutch 8c speakers with a Grace Design m905 and an Avid MTRX, running Pro Tools on a Mac. I also check on AirPods Max and NS10s.

### How do you approach a new mix? Walk us through your first hour on a song.

I import everything into my session and organize it. All my sessions are color-coded the same way, and I like to trim the empty spaces. I go through each track and visually check for clipped waveforms or bleed that shouldn't be there. I route everything through the appropriate buses, then check the stems against the reference the client sent over. I want to be sure I have all the files and I'm not missing any crucial processing or key instruments.

Then I go through the tracks one by one, fixing minor things here and there. Once I've done a first pass, I activate my submaster processing and make sure what I've applied gets close to, or beats, the reference. After that I do a deep dive and tweak everything in detail.

### How has your workflow changed over the last few years?

It has become more granular in the sense that I have more submasters, but I also group more individual audio tracks together when they belong to the same element. Take a choir of 20 vocals: in the early days I would have put processing on each one. Now I group them and process them together, and I think it glues everything together better. On the flip side, having more submasters for the different elements gives me more flexibility.

### What's the most common mistake you hear in mixes sent to you?

Gain staging, from a mix perspective. Having the limiter do too much, or going into the master way too hot.

## Plugins

### What are the 5 plugins you can't live without, and what do you use each one for?

These five are on every session:

**[FabFilter Pro-Q 3](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-3)**
I prefer it over the [Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4) because the high-pass region is a bit bigger on the Q3. I use that all the time, and I find it harder to double-click that on the Q4. Silly, but it just bothered me. I also use the analyzer quite a bit, for example to quickly check muddy regions on instruments against the vocals.

**[PSP VintageWarmer2](/developers/psp-audioware/psp-vintage-warmer-2)**
I've had this plugin for ages and I like what it does. It gives the sound a bit of juice without changing the timbre too much. I usually set the drive to around +5 and the compressor speed to around 80, then adjust the knee to get around 1 to 3 dB of compression. Sometimes I use the high and low EQ as well, just to nudge the sound a bit.

**Softube FET Compressor**
My go-to vocal compressor. I also like the [Tube-Tech CL 1B](/developers/softube/tube-tech-cl-1b), but there's just something about the FET Compressor that I really like. 3:1 ratio, fairly low attack, fast release, and no more than 3 dB of compression (the vocals I get are usually already processed). If the compressor takes too much life out of a vocal, I bring some dry signal back with the mix dial.

**auxCord (Mountainroad DSP)**
My own plugin, which I use on every mix. It streams audio wirelessly between my DAW and my phone, so I can check every mix on the phone speakers. They sometimes reveal harsh frequencies that don't catch my ear on my studio setup.

**[Thermionic Culture Vulture](/developers/universal-audio/thermionic-culture-vulture)**
On drums: around 60% mix, drive at 4, bias at 4, on P1.

### Is there a plugin you bought and never really used?

Yes, there are always plugins I buy and don't really use. Not because they're bad, but because they don't fit my workflow. Also, whenever I receive Pro Tools sessions, there are always a bunch of plugins on there that I don't own, and I end up buying them just so I can open the session in the future. So I have a ton of stuff I don't really use.

## Advice

### What advice would you give to a young engineer starting out today?

It's such a great time to learn about mixing, because there's so much information online. When I started college in 2007, there was basically nothing out there. I remember we had to write a paper on a mix engineer, and it was nearly impossible to find anything. Not just in-depth interviews, but even the names of who mixed what. Information was extremely sparse, and what mixers actually did was pretty much top secret. Today you can go on YouTube deep dives and really learn a lot.

What I would say is: listen to what people have to say, but take it with a grain of salt. When I find a new technique or a new plugin, I always A/B it against my proven setup. It rarely beats what I already have. That's not to say mine is the best, just that not everything new is necessarily better.
