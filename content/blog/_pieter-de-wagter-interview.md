Pieter De Wagter is a mastering engineer in Belgium and the man behind EQuuS. He has been mastering for more than 25 years, works on over 3,000 projects a year, and mastered Stromae's *Racine Carrée*, the album of *Papaoutai* and *Formidable*. Before all that, he was a DJ, and he still owns more than 15,000 CDs and records.

We talked about his first job editing classical music, what restoration taught him about mastering, the nine mixes of *Papaoutai*, why mastering has become a coaching job, his analog chain, and the handful of plugins he actually uses.

## Career

### How did you get into audio, and how did you end up running EQuuS?

From a very young age I've been into music and records, 12 inches especially. I studied piano and flute, then went to a film and sound engineering school. I was not very good with the technical side, but I was immediately hired by a big mastering studio because I spoke several languages and could read music sheets. My first job was to edit classical music and type the PQ listings for the PCM-1630 U-Matic masters (for those who know).

After a few months, I was responsible for the remastering of old catalogue titles and compilations. This allowed me to fine-tune my ears and learn about the history of recorded music. The mastering studio was then bought by a company more concerned about the machines than about the sound engineers, so I followed a colleague who had set up a sound restoration studio: EQuuS. Ten years later, we decided to split our activities, and I kept the company name.

### You started out as a DJ. What did those years teach you that you still use in mastering today?

From DJing I learned to "read the room". I think I know how people will react to certain songs and sounds. I learned how to sequence a mix, or an album. I also know a lot about music history, labels, styles and (re)mixes.

Today, labels looking for a certain track or mix know they can contact me for information (I own more than 15,000 CDs and vinyls). For example, I sometimes tell a label they are using the album version instead of the "hit" radio remix. All of this is part of my service as a mastering engineer. It's not just about the loudness.

### EQuuS started as a restoration facility. Has working alongside restoration shaped the way you master?

What a lot of engineers are missing these days are good mentors. When I started, I was able to join an existing studio, learn from my colleagues, and get the time to experiment and get better. All this while getting paid.

Working with my colleague Marc Doutrepont taught me how far to go when restoring a track. Knowing who the recording is meant for: a wider audience that prefers a pristine sound, maybe with a bit less detail? Or purists who prefer the performance over a perfect sound? Once again, a little piece of the puzzle for a good mastering engineer.

### How has the Belgian music scene changed since you started?

A lot, much like the music scene in the rest of the world. Streaming has enabled anyone to make music and release it. That is great. Unfortunately, everyone and his grandmother is releasing music. A label used to be a kind of filter: quality control from people who invested money and wanted quality.

Now, most artists don't have the money and do everything themselves. So I receive a lot of mixes that aren't good enough to release: out of phase, in mono, too compressed... and that's just the technical side. My job is now to try to salvage a mix and help clients avoid making mistakes. It's sometimes more of a consulting and coaching job.

These are all things AI is not helping with (yet).

### What does a typical week look like for you today?

I work on more than 3,000 projects a year, so I don't have a set schedule: it changes throughout the day. I usually start around 8am with the "easy" stuff: revisions, copies, a jazz album or a soundtrack. At about 10am I start working on the more complex stuff. From 4 to 6pm I go back to the jazz. In between, there's usually a client coming in for a session. I never stop (not even for lunch), but I love it.

### You mastered Stromae's *Racine Carrée*. What do you remember most from that project?

Not the mastering itself, but everything going on while I was mastering the album. The master took just a couple of hours, without any revisions. But the mix was changed multiple times: while I was mastering, they were still figuring out some loops, guitars, percussion... *Papaoutai*, for example, took more than nine masters. One mastering, but nine different mixes.

People also tend to forget that *Papaoutai* was a very different kind of pop song from what the French market was used to: some spoken word, some singing, African guitars, big EDM synths... So radio stations were not sure they wanted to play it. It wasn't until Stromae released *Formidable*, with its viral video, that they began to play *Papaoutai* as well. Both singles went to number 1 and 2 in the charts, and that set the wheels in motion for the success of the rest of the album.

It also shows how important a good mix is. My job as a mastering engineer is to preserve the work my clients have put into their art. Not to destroy it with overly loud and harsh mastering.

## Workflow

### Walk us through your room and your chain: what's the analog/digital balance today?

As is the case for many engineers, I think, I didn't build my room from scratch. I had the opportunity to rent a space at my brother's house, 10 minutes from my own home. But it's basically a cube, so it was not easy to turn it into a real mastering studio. It's not perfect, but I know the room very well.

I'm also lucky enough to work on releases that get played on the radio, so I can listen to my masters on the way to the studio, or at festivals. I use PMC monitors and, apart from a Bluetooth speaker, I don't need extra speakers.

I rely on analog gear: Knif, Maselec and Dangerous Music EQs, Tube-Tech, Thermionic Culture and Dangerous Music compressors, a LAAL limiter and a Whitestone P331. Conversion is done with Lavry Savitr and Quintessence units. These give the masters the colour I'm looking for.

The signal then goes through my plugins. Once again, a limited set of EQs and compressors that I know well, plus some more "exotic" stuff I only use once a year.

The analog master is recorded, but not the plugins: they are added during the final rendering. So for every master I have two versions, a pure analog one and one with the plugins added. This allows for faster revisions, and vinyl masters if necessary.

### What do you listen for first when a mix lands? What makes you say "this one's easy" or "this one's going to be work"?

I'm a music enthusiast more than a technical nerd, so I always rely on my first gut impression of the mix and the song. It has to hit immediately. I only need a few seconds to form a sound in my head of how it should sound.

That's also where my years of DJing and mastering compilations come in handy: I can usually spot the references in the mix. It sounds like The Weeknd, or Tame Impala, or a bit like Fcukers, Derrick May or Stromae.

A good mix will just need some tweaking, and it will be a case of preserving all the good work the mix engineer has already done. I also try not to spend too much time on a master. The longer I work on it, the more I drift from my original intention and feeling about it.

### What's the most common problem in the mixes you receive, and what would you tell mix engineers to fix before sending?

The biggest problem these days is that there's usually no budget for a producer. So the artist has to communicate directly with the mixing engineer, who is not by definition a producer. A lot of my clients come in with mixes they are not happy with, because they don't sound like they originally intended. Or they do everything themselves in their living room and think the mastering will give them the professional sound and loudness of a Taylor Swift record. As I said earlier, mastering is now more about salvaging a mix than preserving it.

And of course, stem mastering is also a problem, because artists think they don't have to finish a mix. I receive 8 to 16 stems, without even a reference stereo mix.

Three important things for a good master: a perfect mix, a perfect mix and a perfect mix.

## Plugins

### How many plugins do you actually use in mastering? Do you buy new ones often, or do you stick to a small set?

I usually use 4 or 5 plugins on a standard master. They help me with the technical tweaking (a harsh voice, a muddy bass) and, of course, with getting the loudness I want.

A side note: I don't have a loudness target. I master the track until I get a sound I like, and only then do I check the loudness meter. Of course, if I master a techno track and the meter reads -11 LUFS, I'll have to make some corrections. Same with a jazz master at -8 LUFS.

I prefer to stick with a small group of plugins I know very well rather than experiment with new ones. I run a business and work on so many projects that streamlining my workflow is essential. It also gives me the best results. That doesn't mean I use the same plugins on every job: for limiting, I have a set of 3 limiters/maximizers, depending on the style of music or the loudness I want to reach. There's still some room for experimentation.

### Which ones do you rely on, and what for?

My go-to plugins are [FabFilter Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4), [iZotope Ozone](/developers/izotope) (only the Maximizer) and [DMG Audio Limitless](/developers/dmg-audio/limitless). Also Leapwing's StageOne, Sonnox's [Oxford Inflator](/developers/sonnox/oxford-inflator), and ADPTR Audio's [Metric AB](/developers/plugin-alliance/metric-ab) for comparing. None of them is known for an "artistic" colouring or a specific sound.

I use them for the more technical part of mastering. Usually on the master bus, but sometimes already on the original mix, before it goes through the analog gear.

### Is there any piece of gear or software you bought and almost never used?

I'm not going to name analog gear I bought and never used because it wasn't good enough. But I have a few units that are a bit of a luxury, because they are expensive and I don't use them on every project:

- **The LAAL limiter.** A great tool, but it's a real limiter, not a maximizer. So the level is usually too high when I feed it at the end of the analog chain, and I use it at the start of the chain instead, to control the peaks a little. Its stereo enhancer works great.
- **The Whitestone P331**, a tube loading amplifier that adds a bit of colour and grit to the mixes. You don't hear it working until you switch it off.

### What advice would you give a young engineer who thinks the next plugin will fix their sound?

When it comes to plugins, don't let the brand name distract you from what the plugin is really doing. Some free plugins work better than some of the more expensive emulations. And never think an emulation sounds the same as the hardware version. These are two different beasts. They may both be good, but they're not the same.

Also, try to keep your head clear and be objective about what your mix needs. Only then can you search for a plugin solution.

And finally, keep it simple and uncluttered. Know what you have and what it will do. When I need a hammer at home, I usually spend more time looking for it than doing the actual work. It's the same with plugins.

## Pieter's plugins, second-hand

Pieter sticks to a small set of tools. Here is what the developers say about reselling them:

- **[FabFilter Pro-Q 4](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-4)**: about €10 for a License Transfer, bought by the seller in their FabFilter account, then the seller moves the license to the buyer. FabFilter treats each major version as a separate product, so an older [Pro-Q](/developers/fabfilter/pro-q-3) can be sold on its own. [FabFilter transfer rules](/developers/fabfilter).
- **iZotope Ozone**: iZotope transfers licenses for free through its Account Support team. Only the current version is transferred, and the past versions attached to the license are disabled. If the license runs on iLok, the iLok fee applies. [iZotope transfer rules](/developers/izotope) and our [iZotope guide](/blog/how-to-transfer-an-izotope-license).
- **[DMG Audio Limitless](/developers/dmg-audio/limitless)**: £20, paid by the seller through the transfer form on DMG Audio's support page. A transferred license can't be transferred again, and EDU licenses can't be resold. [DMG Audio transfer rules](/developers/dmg-audio).
- **[Sonnox Oxford Inflator](/developers/sonnox/oxford-inflator)**: the seller moves the license in iLok License Manager and pays the iLok fee (capped at $50 for several licenses to the same account), no Sonnox fee. Full licenses only, at least 90 days old, no NFR. [Sonnox transfer rules](/developers/sonnox).
- **[ADPTR Audio Metric AB](/developers/plugin-alliance/metric-ab)**: sold through Plugin Alliance, which says license transfers are not currently possible. [Plugin Alliance transfer rules](/developers/plugin-alliance).

Leapwing Audio isn't in our list yet. When you sell, you can add a missing plugin yourself from the [Sell form](/sell), as long as its developer is in our list. And if you're looking for a used plugin, search for it on [Browse](/browse) and set an alert: we'll email you as soon as someone lists it.
