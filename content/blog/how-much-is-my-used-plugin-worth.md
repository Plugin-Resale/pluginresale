Price a used plugin too high and it sits there for months. Price it too low and you leave money on the table. The trick is to think like a buyer: why would someone pick your license over buying it new?

Here's a simple method.

## 1. Start from the street price, not the list price

The list price on the developer's website is rarely what people actually pay. Many plugins are on sale several times a year, and some are almost always discounted.

Before setting your price, check:

- the **current price** on the developer's website and at a couple of big retailers,
- the **usual sale price**: what it drops to during Black Friday, summer sales or the developer's anniversary sale,
- whether a **sale is running right now**. If the plugin is 70% off new this week, nobody will buy yours at 60% of the list price.

Your reference is the price a patient buyer could realistically get it for new.

## 2. Discount it

A second-hand license has to be cheaper than new to be worth the hassle for the buyer: they have to wait for the transfer, sometimes pay a fee, and deal with a stranger instead of a shop.

There's no official rule, but a common starting point is **somewhere between half and two-thirds of the realistic new price**. Go towards the top of that range for a recent version that's rarely on sale, and towards the bottom for an older version or a plugin that's discounted all the time.

## 3. Account for the transfer fee

Transfer fees vary a lot between developers: free for some, a fixed amount for others, a percentage of the price for a few. iLok transfers carry their own fee too. You can check any developer in our [transfer rules database](/developers).

- **If the seller pays the fee** (the most common case), include it in your price, or accept that it comes out of what you get.
- **If the buyer pays it**, lower your price accordingly: buyers compare the total cost, not just your asking price.
- **No fee at all** is a selling point. Mention it in your description.

## 4. Adjust for the details

A few things push the price up or down:

- **Version.** The latest version is worth more. If upgrading to it costs money, the buyer will factor that in.
- **Update plans.** Some developers sell updates separately. A license with updates included or an active update plan is worth more.
- **Bundles.** A bundle is worth less than the sum of its parts to most buyers, who only want two or three plugins from it. Price it for the plugins people actually want.
- **Demand.** Popular mixing and mastering tools sell fast. Niche instruments take longer to find the right buyer.

## 5. Write a listing that sells

Price isn't everything. A clear listing gets more attention:

- the **exact product and version**,
- what's **included** (expansions, extra content, update plan),
- **who pays the transfer fee** and how long the transfer usually takes,
- a line saying it's a **full license** that you bought yourself.

## A quick example

Say a mixing plugin lists at €200 on the developer's website but goes on sale for €100 a few times a year, and the seller pays a small transfer fee.

- Realistic new price: around €100 (a buyer can just wait for the next sale).
- Second-hand range: around €50 to €65.
- If it's the latest version and there's no sale right now, €65 is fair. If a sale starts next week, €50 will sell faster.

## When it's not selling

If your listing has been up for a few weeks without a buyer, check whether the plugin went on sale in the meantime, and lower your price in small steps. Buyers browsing the used market are usually the patient type: a slightly lower price often makes the difference.
